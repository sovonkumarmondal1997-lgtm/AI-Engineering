## Conventions stated once, used everywhere

- **Claims policy.** Every claim about learning efficacy, hiring, the "best" detector, a contamination rate, a `k`/`ν`/`γ`/`ψ` setting, or a threshold is exactly one of: (a) cited research/documentation tagged `[VERIFY]` with limits; (b) practitioner opinion/rule of thumb tagged `[JUDGEMENT]` or `HEURISTIC`; (c) a verifiable statement about mathematics, code, or the tree; or (d) omitted. **No general ranking of detectors; every detector encodes assumptions about what "anomalous" means, and every empirical comparison is stated as evidence about its generator's anomaly mechanism only.** Durations `[ASSUMPTION]`; sizes `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`.
- **Correctness policy.** Every worked example and key is verified **two independent ways**: (1) a hand derivation in the text, and (2) an independent computation — **exact (`Fraction`/`sympy`/enumeration/quadrature) on the tiny tables wherever possible**, otherwise `numpy`/`scipy`/scikit-learn with a **stated tolerance and reason**, or simulation with stated `n`, seed, replicates, interval. **Every detector, score, and monitor in the module is implemented as a short spec-literal reference and cross-checked against scikit-learn/SciPy on tiny and random inputs, including duplicate points, constant features, ties in scores, `k ≥ n`, all-identical data, and score sign conventions.** A library function is never the sole oracle for a claim about that function.
- **Proof policy.** Where Section 6 says "proof", write it in full: statement, hypotheses, every step justified, closing "where each hypothesis was used". **Heuristics are labelled `HEURISTIC` with their measured behaviour on a stated generator.** Where a result is `CITED [VERIFY]`, say which and give a numerical sanity check.
- **Anomaly-definition convention (stated once; used identically everywhere).** **An *anomaly* is a case generated by a mechanism different from the normal one *as defined for the decision at hand*; "rare" is neither necessary nor sufficient. Three kinds are distinguished everywhere: *point* anomalies (individually unusual), *contextual* anomalies (unusual given a context such as segment or time), and *collective* anomalies (individually ordinary, jointly unusual). Two settings are distinguished everywhere: *outlier detection* (the training data may contain anomalies; contamination `ε` unknown) and *novelty detection* (the training data are assumed clean; new data are screened). Every detector's score is oriented so that **larger = more anomalous** in the learner's code (library sign conventions are converted and tested).**
- **Planted-truth convention.** Every detector, threshold rule, and monitor is evaluated on generators for which **the anomaly labels and the anomaly mechanism are known, the inlier density (hence the likelihood-ratio-optimal oracle score where the anomaly law is specified) is computable, and the true false-alarm rate of any threshold on inliers is computable by quadrature or by a very large independent inlier sample**, so that **detection quality (ROC-AUC, average precision, precision@`K`, detection rate at a fixed false-alarm rate), threshold false-alarm and detection rates, and monitor false-alarm and delay are measured against truth over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds with intervals**, never inferred from the data the method saw. **The no-anomaly null generator is part of every study (any "detection" there is a false alarm).**
- **Evaluation-protocol and leak-free convention (inherited from 5A-00 to 5A-13 and the 5B protocol card).** Every learned object — scalers, reductions, the detector, **its hyperparameters, the contamination setting, the threshold, calibration of scores, and the choice of detector** — is fitted on the rows belonging to its role: **train (the detector; for novelty detection, clean rows) / validation (hyperparameters, detector choice, threshold: *the only place labels may be used for selection*) / calibration (`X_cal`: conformal p-values; clean inlier rows) / test (sealed; touched once)**. **Labels are scarce: every use of labels for selection is counted, and the same labelled anomalies are never used to both select and evaluate (nesting or disjoint label sets: `../01-…`).** **In outlier-detection mode the detector may be fitted on contaminated rows, but evaluation rows are never fitted; in novelty mode, any contamination of the training set is a stated violation.** **The unit of independence (row, entity, time block) is stated; entities and time respect the splits.** Every example states which items it exercises; **an example that violates one is labelled as a counterexample.**

## Unit 1 — Anomalies, outliers, and novelty

- **L1:** **an anomaly is a case produced by a different mechanism from the normal ones — what counts as "different" depends on the decision (a fraudulent payment, a failing machine, a defective part); *rare* is not the same as *anomalous* (many rare cases are fine) and *anomalous* is not always rare**; **three kinds, with examples: *point* anomalies (a transaction ten times larger than any other), *contextual* anomalies (a normal amount at an unusual time, or a normal temperature for the wrong season), *collective* anomalies (each reading looks normal but the sequence does not)**; **two settings: *outlier detection* (you have a dataset that may contain some anomalies and want to find them) and *novelty detection* (you trust a dataset of normal cases and want to screen new cases against it); the same algorithm can be used in both but the evaluation and the risks differ**; **the output: a *score* (how anomalous) and, if you want alerts, a *threshold* on the score; choosing the threshold is a decision about false alarms and capacity (Unit 7)**; **a first worked example by hand: the nine-point 2-D table (eight inliers, one anomaly): the anomaly under three simple scores (distance from the mean, distance to the nearest neighbour, rank by each) and what each assumes; then a contextual case where the anomaly is invisible to every marginal score**; **why this is hard: there are usually few or no labels, "anomalous" is task-defined, and every detector is a guess about what anomalies look like**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 2 — Statistical and distance-based methods

- **L1:** **the simplest detectors measure how far a case is from the typical ones: *how many standard deviations from the mean* (z-score), *how far outside the middle half of the data* (the IQR fence), *how far in a way that accounts for correlated features* (Mahalanobis distance), and *how far to the nearest other cases* (k-nearest-neighbour distance)**; **robust versions: the mean and standard deviation are themselves dragged by anomalies, so use the median and the *median absolute deviation* (MAD) instead (the "modified z-score")**; **a first worked example by hand: a nine-point 1-D table: the z-scores (the anomaly inflates the standard deviation and hides itself), the modified z-scores (the anomaly stands out), the IQR fences; then a 2-D table with correlated features where a point is ordinary on each axis but far in Mahalanobis distance**; **scaling matters: distances in unscaled features are dominated by the largest-scale feature (5A-05 Unit 5)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **statistical and distance methods in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) univariate rules** (z-score; **modified z-score** `0.6745(x − med)/MAD` with the consistency constant `1/Φ^{-1}(3/4) = 1.4826` (so that `1.4826·MAD` estimates `σ` for Gaussian data); IQR fences `[Q1 − 1.5 IQR, Q3 + 1.5 IQR]` and their false-alarm rate under normality (`0.0070`: derived in L3); Grubbs' and generalised ESD tests named `CITED [VERIFY]`; per-feature rules and multiplicity across `p` features (the chance that *some* feature of an inlier trips a per-feature rule is `1 − (1 − α)^p`: a table); skew and heavy tails: transform first); **(2) Mahalanobis distance** (`D²(x) = (x − μ)ᵀΣ^{-1}(x − μ)`; under a Gaussian with known `μ, Σ`, `D² ~ χ²_p` so the false-alarm rate of the threshold `χ²_{p,1−α}` is `α` (derived in L3); with *estimated* `μ̂, Σ̂` from `n` rows the distribution is a scaled Beta/`F` form `CITED [VERIFY]` and the `χ²` threshold is anti-conservative for small `n/p`: measured; **masking by contamination**: the non-robust covariance is inflated by anomalies and the Mahalanobis distances of the anomalies shrink (measured vs the Unit 1 formula); **robust covariance** by the **minimum covariance determinant** (MCD: the covariance of the `h`-subset with the smallest determinant; `MinCovDet`, `support_fraction`, `EllipticEnvelope` `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`; breakdown point up to 50%; cost and `n > p` requirements; the learner implements a simple C-step iteration with random starts and compares with the library); the limits: ellipsoidal (unimodal Gaussian-like) inliers only; multimodal inliers break it: a measured example); **(3) distance-to-neighbours scores** (`k`-NN distance (distance to the `k`th neighbour) and mean distance to the `k` nearest; the effect of `k` (small `k`: sensitive to duplicates and micro-clusters; **varying density** makes global k-NN thresholds unfair to sparse-but-normal regions (the motivation for LOF: Unit 4)); **(4) the curse of dimensionality** (distance concentration (4B-04, 5A-05 Unit 5): in high dimension with irrelevant features all k-NN distances look alike and the detection quality of distance methods falls: a seeded study of AUC vs the number of noise features with intervals, and the benefit of feature selection or reduction (`../05-…`) as denoising — evidence only); **(5) one-class and mixture-of-Gaussian generalisations** (a single Gaussian per context/segment (conditional baselines for contextual anomalies); Gaussian mixtures: Unit 6); **(6) computation** (`O(np²)` fit and `O(p²)` per score for Mahalanobis; - **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the IQR fence false-alarm rate under normality: with `Q1 = −z σ`, `Q3 = zσ` for `z = Φ^{-1}(0.75) = 0.6745`, `IQR = 1.349σ`, the fences are `±(0.6745 + 1.5·1.349)σ = ±2.698σ` and the probability of falling outside is `2(1 − Φ(2.698)) = 0.00698` (computed; verified by `mpmath`)** — complete; (b) **MAD consistency: for `X ~ N(μ, σ²)`, `median|X − μ| = σΦ^{-1}(3/4) = 0.6745σ`, so `MAD/0.6745 = 1.4826·MAD` is a consistent estimator of `σ`; hence the modified z-score `0.6745(x − med)/MAD` has unit scale under normality** — complete; (c) **breakdown points: the sample mean and standard deviation can be made arbitrarily large by replacing a single observation (breakdown point `1/n → 0`); the median and MAD break down only when `⌈n/2⌉` observations are replaced (breakdown ≈ 50%) — proof: if fewer than half the points are moved, the median stays within the range of the unmoved points, and the same for the MAD** — complete; (d) **Mahalanobis distribution: if `x ~ N(μ, Σ)` then `Σ^{-1/2}(x − μ) ~ N(0, I_p)` so `D²(x) = ‖Σ^{-1/2}(x − μ)‖² ~ χ²_p`; the Mahalanobis distance is invariant to invertible affine transformations of the features (`D²` unchanged under `x ↦ Ax + b`)** — complete; (e) **per-feature multiplicity: with `p` independent per-feature rules each with false-alarm rate `α`, the inlier false-alarm rate of the "any feature" rule is `1 − (1 − α)^p`; for `α = 0.007`, `p = 20` it is `0.131` (computed)** — complete; ## Unit 3 — Isolation Forest

- **L1:** **Isolation Forest scores a case by *how quickly random cuts isolate it*: build many random trees, each by repeatedly choosing a random feature and a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum; anomalies, being few and different, tend to be separated from the rest in *few* cuts (a short path from the root), whereas normal points need many cuts**; **the score: the average path length over the trees, converted to a number between 0 and 1 where *larger means more anomalous* (the library reports its negative: a sign convention you will test)**; **why random trees on *subsamples* work: each tree sees a small sample (256 by default), which makes isolation easier for anomalies and the method fast; no distance or density is computed**; **a first worked example by hand: the nine-point 2-D table; three random trees drawn with stated random choices; the path length of the anomaly and of two inliers in each tree; the average; the normalising constant `c(n)` and the final score `2^{−E[h]/c(n)}`**; **what to remember: the score depends on the random seed (average over many trees); axis-parallel cuts create artefacts; it is not a probability**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **Isolation Forest in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the algorithm** (subsample `ψ` rows without replacement per tree; build an isolation tree: stop at depth limit `⌈log₂ψ⌉` or when a node has one row (or identical rows); random feature, uniform random split in `[min, max]`; path length `h(x)` = depth of the leaf plus the adjustment `c(size)` for unexpanded leaves; forest of `t` trees (default 100); the score `s(x, ψ) = 2^{−E[h(x)]/c(ψ)}`; **`c(n) = 2H(n − 1) − 2(n − 1)/n`** with `H(i) ≈ ln i + 0.5772` the average path length of an unsuccessful binary-search-tree lookup (derived in L3); `c(256) ≈ 10.24`); **(2) the score's interpretation** (`s ≈ 0.5` when the average path equals `c(ψ)` (nothing stands out); `decision_function = score_samples − offset_`; `predict` uses `offset_` from `contamination`: tested); **(3) why subsampling helps and what it costs** (swamping and masking are reduced because the subsample contains fewer anomalies and inliers near them; too small `ψ` loses structure; too large increases masking and cost; a measured study of AUC/AP vs `ψ` and `t` on planted generators with intervals — evidence only; variance of the score across seeds as a function of `t`: measured; the learner reports the number of trees needed for a stable *ranking* (rank-correlation across seeds) on the problem, not a rule); **(4) feature handling and the axis-parallel bias** (random axis-aligned splits create **ghost regions**: points on the axes' extensions of the data cloud receive artificially short paths/low scores in correlated data; a planted correlated-data example with a measured score map as a table; rotation of the features changes scores (the method is not rotation-invariant): measured; remedies named: **Extended Isolation Forest** (random hyperplane splits) `CITED [VERIFY]` and rotated/PCA-preprocessed features (`../05-…`), each evaluated, not assumed); **(5) irrelevant features and scaling** (the method is invariant to *affine* rescaling of single features but *not* to general monotone transformations (the spacing of values matters): proved in L3; irrelevant features dilute splits: measured robustness vs noise dimensions compared with distance-based methods on the same generators — evidence only); **(6) categorical and mixed data** (encodings; one-hot dilution; named options); **(7) contamination and the threshold** (the library's `contamination` sets the threshold at the corresponding quantile of the *training* scores: Unit 1(d); better threshold rules: Unit 7); **(8) explainability** (per-feature contribution via the proportion of splits on a feature along the path (named `CITED [VERIFY]`), a simple implemented version as alert context; limits); **(9) complexity and streaming** (`O(tψ log ψ)` fit and `O(t log ψ)` per score: counted; independence of `n` for a fixed `ψ`; model refresh on streams (named windows: Unit 8)); **(10) planted-truth evaluation** (point, contextual (via context features), collective anomalies; AUC/AP/detection at fixed false-alarm rate; oracle gap; **no ranking**; failure cases); **(11) reporting** (`ψ`, `t`, `max_features`, seeds, score orientation, threshold, stability of ranking, ghost-region checks)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the normalising constant: `c(n) = 2H(n − 1) − 2(n − 1)/n` is the average depth of the `n` external nodes (unsuccessful-search endpoints) of a random binary search tree built from `n − 1` keys inserted in uniformly random order, with `H(i)` the `i`th harmonic number (`H(i) ≈ ln i + 0.5772`); **counterexamples**: **a ghost-region point scoring more anomalous than a true anomaly (computed/seeded)**; **a score change after rotating the features (computed)**; **an Isolation Forest with `ψ` too small missing a micro-cluster of anomalies and with `ψ` too large masking it (seeded; ## Unit 4 — Local outlier factor

- **L1:** **LOF asks: *is this point much less dense than its neighbours?* — so a point in a sparse region is not suspicious *if its neighbours are also sparse* (a thin but normal cluster), but is suspicious if it sits in a thin patch next to dense neighbours**; **the idea in plain words: estimate each point's *local density* from the distances to its `k` nearest neighbours; compare it with the local densities of those neighbours; the *local outlier factor* is the neighbours' average density divided by the point's own: about 1 means "like its neighbours", clearly above 1 means "sparser than its neighbours"**; **a first worked example by hand: the ten-point varying-density table (a tight cluster, a loose cluster, and one point just outside the tight cluster): the `k`-distances, the reachability distances, the local reachability densities, and the LOF values (computed with `Fraction`), compared with the plain k-NN distance score, which flags the whole loose cluster**; **LOF is for *varying density*; its output is a ratio, not a probability, and `k` matters**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **LOF in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the definitions** (`k`-distance of `a`: distance to its `k`th nearest neighbour; `N_k(a)`: the `k`-neighbourhood (ties can make it larger than `k`); **reachability distance** `reach-dist_k(a, b) = max{k-dist(b), d(a, b)}`; **local reachability density** `lrd_k(a) = 1/[(1/|N_k(a)|)Σ_{b∈N_k(a)}reach-dist_k(a, b)]`; **`LOF_k(a) = (1/|N_k(a)|)Σ_{b∈N_k(a)}lrd_k(b)/lrd_k(a)`); **(2) interpretation** (≈ 1 inside a region of roughly uniform density; `> 1` outliers; `< 1` points denser than their neighbours; the library's `negative_outlier_factor_` is `−LOF` (sign convention `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`: tested); there is no universal cutoff: values near 1.0–1.1 are normal on some data and 1.5 can be normal on others (a measured null distribution on inlier-only generators: the *distribution of LOF under no anomalies*, and the threshold from its quantile with a false-alarm interval)); **(3) choosing `k`** (`k` below the size of an anomalous cluster lets the cluster hide itself; `k` above the size of the smallest normal cluster makes that cluster look anomalous; a seeded study of AUC vs `k` for point and small-cluster anomalies on multi-density generators with intervals; a *range* of `k` aggregated by the maximum LOF (named in the original method `CITED [VERIFY]`) and its measured effect — **no universal `k`**); **(4) outlier versus novelty mode** (`fit_predict`/`negative_outlier_factor_` on the training data themselves (outlier mode: each point is excluded from its own neighbourhood) versus `novelty=True` with `predict`/`score_samples`/`decision_function` on *new* data only after fitting on clean data `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`; the learner's implementation of both modes; using outlier-mode scores on held-out rows is a bug: measured); **(5) duplicates and degenerate cases** (identical points make reach distances zero and `lrd = ∞`: LOF becomes `∞/∞` or 0; remedies: jitter, deduplication with weights, a minimum reachability floor; implemented and tested); **(6) dimension, scale, and the metric** (distance concentration (Unit 2); scaling is essential; metric choice; the effect of irrelevant features on AUC: measured); **(7) complexity** (`O(n²p)` brute force for neighbours; indexed structures in low dimension; memory `O(nk)`; guards); **(8) LOF versus global k-NN on varying-density data** (planted multi-density generators: where each wins, with intervals — **evidence only; no ranking**); **(9) planted-truth evaluation** (point anomalies near dense clusters; anomalies between clusters; small anomalous clusters; contextual anomalies; AUC/AP/detection at fixed false-alarm rate; oracle gap); **(10) reporting** (`k`, neighbour search, mode, duplicates policy, null distribution of LOF, threshold, failure cases)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **scale invariance: multiplying all distances by `c > 0` multiplies every reachability distance by `c`, every `lrd` by `1/c`, and leaves every LOF unchanged (ratios of `lrd`s)** — complete; (b) **LOF ≈ 1 in a uniform-density region (statement with proof for the idealised case): if all points in `N_k(a)` and `a` itself have equal `lrd`, then `LOF_k(a) = 1`; for points drawn from a homogeneous Poisson process the expected `LOF` is `≈ 1` (an asymptotic statement `CITED [VERIFY]`; verified by simulation)** — complete for the equal-`lrd` case; `CITED` for the Poisson statement; (c) **the Breunig et al. bounds: if `direct_min`, `direct_max` are the minimum and maximum reachability distances from `a` to its neighbours and `indirect_min`, `indirect_max` those from the neighbours to *their* neighbours, then `direct_min/indirect_max ≤ LOF(a) ≤ direct_max/indirect_min`; proof: `lrd(a) ∈ [1/direct_max, 1/direct_min]` and `lrd(b) ∈ [1/indirect_max, 1/indirect_min]`, then take the ratio** — complete (the bounds follow from the definitions; the statement of the original paper's full theorem is `CITED`); (d) **why reachability distance smooths: replacing `d(a, b)` by `max{k-dist(b), d(a, b)}` makes all points within `k-dist(b)` of `b` equally "reachable" from `b`, reducing the variance of `lrd` estimates in dense clusters (a computed variance comparison on a uniform 1-D example)** — complete (a computed example); (e) **global k-NN fails where LOF succeeds: a computed one-dimensional example with a tight cluster `{0, 0.1, …, 0.9}` and a loose cluster `{10, 12, …, 20}` and a point `x = 1.9`: the 3-NN distance of the loose cluster's members (≈ 4–6) exceeds that of `1.9` (≈ 1.0–1.8), so the global k-NN score ranks the whole loose cluster above the anomaly, while `LOF(1.9) > 1.5` and `LOF(loose points) ≈ 1`; hence LOF is ill-defined; with a floor `ε` on reachability distance LOF is finite and scale-dependent on `ε` (a computed example)** — complete; **counterexamples**: **global k-NN flagging a normal loose cluster (computed)**; **a small anomalous cluster of size `k + 1` hiding from LOF at small `k` (computed/seeded)**; **a normal small cluster flagged at large `k` (seeded)**; **outlier-mode scores applied to new data (seeded bug)**; **LOF `= ∞` with duplicates (computed)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal LOF (outlier and novelty modes) with ties and duplicates handled by stated rules vs `LocalOutlierFactor` on tiny and random data (agreement conditions: neighbourhood ties, `algorithm`, sign)**; **tool card** for `anomlab.lof` (LOF in outlier and novelty modes with explicit tie and duplicate policies; `k`-range aggregation; null-distribution estimation on inlier-only generators; threshold-from-quantile with false-alarm intervals; ## Unit 5 — One-class SVM

- **L1:** **a one-class SVM learns a *boundary around the normal data* in a (kernel-mapped) feature space: it finds a function that is positive for most training points and negative outside, by pushing the boundary as far from the origin as it can while allowing a small fraction `ν` of the training points to fall outside**; **the key parameters: the kernel (usually RBF) and its width `γ` (how local the boundary is), and `ν ∈ (0, 1]` (an upper bound on the fraction of training points allowed outside and a lower bound on the fraction of *support vectors*)**; **what you get: a decision function (positive = inside = normal); a score you can threshold; no probability**; **a first worked example by hand: a six-point 1-D training set with an RBF kernel (`γ` stated) and `ν = 1/3`: the dual solution found by a few projected-gradient steps (or by `Fraction`-exact solve of the small QP), the support vectors, the offset `ρ`, and the decision values at three test points; then the same with a tiny `γ` (boundary far too wide) and a huge `γ` (every point its own island)**; **warnings: features must be scaled; the default `gamma='scale'` is a heuristic, not a tuned value; the result depends strongly on `γ`**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **the one-class SVM in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the formulation** (Schölkopf's ν-one-class SVM: `min ½‖w‖² + (1/νn)Σξ_i − ρ` subject to `⟨w, φ(x_i)⟩ ≥ ρ − ξ_i`, `ξ_i ≥ 0`; decision function `f(x) = sign(Σα_iK(x_i, x) − ρ)`; the dual: `min ½ΣΣα_iα_jK(x_i, x_j)` subject to `0 ≤ α_i ≤ 1/(νn)`, `Σα_i = 1`; support vectors; the offset from any `0 < α_i < 1/(νn)`); **(2) the ν property** (**the fraction of training points outside the boundary is at most `ν` and the fraction of support vectors is at least `ν`**: proved in L3; `ν` is *not* the anomaly rate and *not* the exact outlier fraction: a measured demonstration on a no-anomaly generator and on contaminated data); **(3) the kernel and `γ`** (RBF `K(x, y) = exp(−γ‖x − y‖²)`; `γ` as inverse squared length-scale; **`gamma='scale'`** = `1/(p·Var(X))` `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`: tested; the memorisation regime (large `γ`: every training point is a support vector, the boundary hugs the data and *everything* new looks anomalous) and the over-smoothing regime (small `γ`: the boundary is a large blob); selecting `γ` and `ν` with scarce labels (nested: `../01-…`; **never on the evaluation labels**) or by a *novelty-consistency* criterion on held-out clean rows (the fraction of held-out inliers inside the boundary should be near `1 − ν`: a measured check); **(4) scaling and features** (distance-based like k-NN: scaling is mandatory; irrelevant features dilute the kernel: measured); **(5) relation to density and to SVDD** (for the RBF kernel (constant `K(x, x)`) the one-class SVM is equivalent to support vector data description (SVDD: the minimum enclosing ball in feature space) `CITED [VERIFY]`; the decision function is related to a kernel density estimate with weights `α_i` (the weighted Parzen sum): `Σα_iK(x_i, x)` — an interpretation, not a probability density); **(6) the solver** (the learner implements a small dual solver (projected gradient with the simplex-box projection, or an SMO-style pair update) for `n ≤ 500`, compared with `OneClassSVM` on tiny and random inputs (agreement conditions: tolerance, kernel parameters); `O(n²)`–`O(n³)` cost and `O(n²)` memory computed before allocation; `SGDOneClassSVM` with kernel approximation named for scale `CITED [VERIFY]`); **(7) outlier versus novelty use** (trained on contaminated data the boundary bends toward anomalies; `ν` as a robustness knob: measured; novelty mode with clean training data is the natural use); **(8) planted-truth evaluation** (AUC/AP/detection at fixed false-alarm rate for point and cluster anomalies, multi-modal inliers, noise dimensions, and contamination; sensitivity grids over `γ` and `ν` with intervals; oracle gap; **no ranking**; failure cases); **(9) calibration of the score** (the decision values are not probabilities; mapping to probabilities requires labels (Platt/isotonic on small sets: `../04-…` with its risks) or the conformal route of Unit 7); **(10) reporting** (kernel, `γ`, `ν`, scaling, solver, support-vector fraction, held-out inlier acceptance, threshold, failure cases)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the ν property from the dual: constraints `0 ≤ α_i ≤ 1/(νn)` and `Σα_i = 1`; a training point with `ξ_i > 0` (outside the boundary) has `α_i = 1/(νn)` (complementary slackness), so if `n_out` points are outside then `n_out/(νn) ≤ Σα_i = 1`, i.e., `n_out/n ≤ ν`; a support vector has `α_i > 0` and `α_i ≤ 1/(νn)`, so `n_SV/(νn) ≥ Σα_i = 1`, i.e., `n_SV/n ≥ ν`** — complete (KKT conditions assumed; derive the complementary-slackness statement from the Lagrangian); (b) **the dual derivation: form the Lagrangian with multipliers `α_i` (for the margin constraints) and `β_i` (for `ξ_i ≥ 0`); stationarity in `w`, `ξ`, and `ρ` gives `w = Σα_iφ(x_i)`, `α_i = 1/(νn) − β_i ≤ 1/(νn)`, and `Σα_i = 1`; substitution yields the stated dual** — complete; (c) **for the RBF kernel `K(x, x) = 1`, all `φ(x_i)` lie on the unit sphere in feature space, `Σα_i = 1` and the objective `½ΣΣα_iα_jK_{ij}` equals `½‖Σα_iφ(x_i)‖²`; hence the solution is the point of minimum norm in the convex hull of the (capped) `φ(x_i)`, and the decision function is `⟨w, φ(x)⟩ − ρ`; the geometric picture and the equivalence with SVDD (statement `CITED [VERIFY]`)** — complete for the norm-minimisation form; (d) **memorisation limit: as `γ → ∞`, `K(x_i, x_j) → δ_{ij}` for distinct points; the dual becomes `min ½Σα_i²` with `Σα_i = 1`, `α_i ≤ 1/(νn)`, minimised by `α_i = 1/n` (for `ν ≤ 1`), so every point is a support vector and the decision value at a new point `x ≠ x_i` is `Σα_iK(x_i, x) − ρ → −ρ < 0`: every new point is flagged** — complete; (e) **over-smoothing limit: as `γ → 0`, `K → 1` and the decision function becomes constant; the boundary degenerates** — complete; (f) **`gamma='scale'`: `γ = 1/(p·Var(X))` with `Var` the variance of the *entire* data matrix (all entries) `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`: for standardised features `Var = 1` and `γ = 1/p`; the typical squared distance between two independent standard-normal points in `p` dimensions is `2p`, so `γK` exponent `= γ‖x − y‖² ≈ 2`: the kernel value at a typical pair is `e^{−2} ≈ 0.135` (computed)** — complete (arithmetic); the default is a heuristic; (g) **projection onto the capped simplex: the Euclidean projection of `v` onto `{α : 0 ≤ α_i ≤ u, Σα_i = 1}` is `α_i = clip(v_i − τ, 0, u)` with `τ` the unique solution of `Σclip(v_i − τ, 0, u) = 1` (monotone in `τ`: bisection); ## Unit 6 — Density- and reconstruction-based methods

- **L1:** **two more ways to define "normal": (1) *density*: model how likely each region of the data is and call low-likelihood points anomalous (a Gaussian, a mixture of Gaussians, a kernel density estimate); (2) *reconstruction*: learn a compact description of normal data (for example, the main principal components) and call a point anomalous if the compact description *cannot reconstruct it well* (a large residual)**; **a first worked example by hand: the nine-point 2-D table: the fitted Gaussian log-density of each point (and its relation to the Mahalanobis distance), the one-component PCA reconstruction error of each point, and the two rankings — an anomaly *along* the main direction is caught by density but not by reconstruction, and an anomaly *off* the main direction is caught by both**; **the high-dimension warning in one line: in many dimensions, *typical* points are not near the density's peak, so "highest density" is not the same as "most normal"**; **reconstruction can be done with PCA (this lesson), or with autoencoders (5C: named)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 7 — Thresholding with scarce labels

- **L1:** **a detector gives scores; an alert system needs a threshold; with few or no labels, "which threshold?" is the hardest part**; **four honest ways to choose one: (1) *capacity*: alert on the top `K` per day (a decision about people's time: recalled from 5B-03 Unit 6); (2) *false-alarm budget*: pick the threshold so that about `α` of *normal* cases are flagged (measured on clean data — needs no anomaly labels); (3) *conformal*: a threshold with a *guaranteed* false-alarm rate for new normal cases, using a held-out set of normal cases; (4) *audited labels*: have a person judge a random sample of flagged and unflagged cases and estimate precision and recall with uncertainty**; **a first worked example by hand: twenty held-out normal scores; the conformal p-value of a new score; the threshold for `α = 0.1` (the index `⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉`, recalled from 5B-02 Unit 5); the expected number of false alarms per 10,000 normal cases (`10,000 × 0.1`)**; **the labels you *do* have (a handful of known anomalies) are precious: use them to *select* a detector or sanity-check a threshold — never to both select and evaluate**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
