- **Correctness policy.** Every worked example and key is verified **two independent ways**: (1) a hand derivation in the text, and (2) an independent computation — **exact (`Fraction`/`sympy`/enumeration/quadrature) on the tiny scored table and small discrete problems wherever possible**, otherwise `numpy`/`scipy`/scikit-learn with a **stated tolerance and reason**, or simulation with stated `n`, seed, replicates, interval. **Every learner, resampler, threshold rule, and simulator in the module is implemented as a short spec-literal reference and cross-checked against scikit-learn/SciPy on tiny and random inputs, including ties in scores, zero positives in a fold, all-positive folds, `k` larger than the minority count, and extreme prevalence.** A library function is never the sole oracle for a claim about that function.

- **Planted-truth convention.** Every imbalance technique and threshold rule is evaluated on generators for which **the true posterior `P(y = 1 | x)`, the true optimal decision rule, and the true deployment cost, precision, and recall of any scored model are computable** (from the known class-conditional densities, or from a very large independent sample with its interval), so that **optimism, bias, regret (cost above the oracle-threshold cost), and miss rates are measured against truth over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds**, never inferred from the data the method saw. **The null generator and a high-overlap generator are part of every study.**
- **Evaluation-protocol and leak-free convention (inherited from 5A-00 to 5A-13 and the 5B protocol card).** Every learned object — preprocessing, the model, **resampled or synthetic rows, class weights derived from data, calibration maps, the threshold** — is fitted on the rows belonging to its role: **train / validation (threshold and hyperparameter selection) / calibration (`X_cal`) / test**; **resampling and synthetic-row generation happen only inside the training portion of each fold, never before the split, and never on validation, calibration, or test rows; the validation and test sets keep the deployment class distribution (or are corrected by an explicitly stated weighting)**; the test set is sealed, touched once, and logged. **The unit of independence (row, entity, time block) is stated; with entities, resampling and splitting respect it.** Every example states which items it exercises; **an example that violates one is labelled as a counterexample.**

## Unit 1 — Rare-event problem framing

- **L1:** **a rare-event problem is one where the event you care about happens in a small fraction `π` of cases (the *prevalence* or *base rate*): a fraud in one transaction of a thousand, a failure in one machine-week of a hundred**; **why ordinary habits break: (1) accuracy rewards always saying "no event" (`1 − π`); (2) with few positives every estimate about them is noisy; (3) even a very good detector produces mostly *false* alerts because negatives vastly outnumber positives — the *base-rate effect*; (4) the cost of missing an event is usually very different from the cost of a false alarm**; **the confusion table at a threshold (recalled from 5A-06 Unit 2) and the four numbers that matter here: recall (what share of events you catch), precision (what share of alerts are real events), the false-positive rate, and the alert volume**; **the base-rate effect in one formula (derived in L3): precision `= (R·π)/(R·π + FPR·(1 − π))`; a first worked example by hand: `R = 0.9`, specificity `0.99` (FPR `0.01`), `π = 0.001`: the precision is `0.0009/(0.0009 + 0.00999) = 0.0826` (computed with `Fraction`) — about 8% of alerts are real, even though the detector looks excellent**; **how many positives do you have? the number `m` of positive cases (not the total `n`) sets the width of every interval about the rare class: "recall 90% on 10 positives" means something very different from "recall 90% on 1,000"**; **framing questions to ask before modelling: what is the event, exactly, and over what horizon; who acts on an alert and how many can they handle; what do a miss and a false alarm cost; how will labels arrive (and how late); how was the training data sampled**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **rare-event framing in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the event definition and label horizon** (what counts as an event, within which window; label delay and the censoring of recent cases (negatives that are not yet known to be negative); the leakage trap of features computed after the event; a worked example where changing the horizon changes the prevalence and the model); **(2) sampling designs and what they do to prevalence** (cohort/random sampling: `π_s = π`; **stratified sampling** of positives and negatives at different rates; **case–control sampling** (all or most positives plus a sample of negatives): `π_s ≫ π`; consequences: a model fitted on `π_s` has probabilities that are *not* valid for the deployment prior; **prior-shift (intercept) correction** for logistic-type models (derived in L3): subtract `ln[(π_s/(1 − π_s))/(π/(1 − π))]` from the log-odds; evaluating on a case–control test set changes precision and cost (not recall, FPR, or ROC-AUC): a seeded demonstration with planted truth); **(3) metrics that respect rarity** (recalled from 5A-06: PR curve and average precision; ROC-AUC's invariance to prevalence versus PR's dependence on it, proved in L3; **precision/recall at an operating point or at capacity `K`**; lift and gain curves; expected cost; the AP of a random scorer is about `π`, so PR-AUC values must be read against `π`; **never compare PR-AUC across different prevalences without stating them**); **(4) how many positives are enough** (the planning formulas: for a recall half-width `h` around `R`, `m ≳ z²R(1 − R)/h²` (e.g., `R = 0.9`, `h = 0.05`: `m ≈ 139`); the **rule of three** for zero observed events in `n` trials (a 95% upper bound `≈ 3/n`: derived in L3); the number of positives per fold in cross-validation (`m/K`); a table of required `m` for target widths; **measured**: the width of Clopper–Pearson and Wilson intervals for recall at `m = 5, 10, 30, 100, 1000`); **(5) interval methods that respect `m`** (exact Clopper–Pearson and Wilson for recall and precision on their own denominators; **stratified bootstrap** (resample positives and negatives separately; why a plain bootstrap can produce resamples with no positives); the dependence among cross-validation folds (5B-01 Unit 6)); **(6) evaluation splits with few positives** (stratified K-fold; repeated stratified K-fold; stratified group K-fold for entities (`[VERSION-SENSITIVE]` behaviour with few positives); the minimum number of folds so that every fold has at least a stated number of positives; why leave-one-out is wasteful and a single hold-out is dangerous; **a fold with zero positives has undefined recall and breaks naive averaging**); **(7) baselines for rare events** (never-alert; random with prevalence-matched alert rate; a simple rule; a linear model; the cost of each under the decision card; the *do-nothing* cost as the bar); **(8) decision framing** (the decision card of the 5B overview applied: actions per alert; cost matrix with sources; capacity; escalation; the possibility that the right system is a ranked list for humans, not a classifier: Unit 6); **(9) when the framing says do not model** (if capacity is zero, if positives are too few to evaluate anything, or if a rule suffices: honest scoping; link 5A-01 Unit 8); **(10) reporting** (prevalence, `m` per split, sampling design, metrics with `m`-aware intervals, baselines, the decision card)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **precision from rates: for a detector with true-positive rate `R` and false-positive rate `F` on a population of prevalence `π`, `P(y = 1 | alert) = Rπ/(Rπ + F(1 − π))` (Bayes' rule); in odds form `posterior odds = (R/F) × prior odds`; hence precision is increasing in `π`, and for fixed `R, F` it tends to 0 as `π → 0`** — complete; (b) **ROC-AUC does not depend on prevalence while precision does: `AUC = P(S₁ > S₀)` with `S₁ ~` positive scores and `S₀ ~` negative scores, a function of the two class-conditional score distributions only; so resampling the negatives or changing `π` (with fixed class-conditional distributions) leaves AUC unchanged and changes precision according to (a)** — complete; (c) **the AP of a random scorer: for a scorer independent of the label, the expected precision at every recall level equals `π`, so `E[AP] ≈ π` (exactly `π` in the limit; a finite-sample value computed for tiny `n` by enumeration)** — complete for the limit (`E[precision at any depth] = π`), enumeration for tiny `n`; (d) **the rule of three: if no events are observed in `n` independent trials, the exact one-sided 95% (Clopper–Pearson) upper bound `p_U` solves `(1 − p_U)^n = 0.05`, so `p_U = 1 − 0.05^{1/n}`; since `−ln 0.05 = 2.9957`, `p_U ≈ 2.9957/n ≈ 3/n` for large `n`** — complete (with the Taylor step); (e) **variance of recall: with `m` independent positives each caught with probability `R`, `R̂ = TP/m` has variance `R(1 − R)/m`; the Wilson interval's half-width at `R̂ = 0.9` and `m = 10` (computed) is about `0.19`; so a 10-positive validation set cannot distinguish `R = 0.9` from `R = 0.75`** — complete (binomial; computed values); (f) **case–control prior correction: if positives are sampled with probability `a` and negatives with probability `b` (independently of `x`), then `P_s(y = 1 | x)/P_s(y = 0 | x) = (a/b)·P(y = 1 | x)/P(y = 0 | x)`, so for a logistic model `logit P(y = 1 | x) = logit P_s(y = 1 | x) − ln(a/b)`; the slope parameters are unaffected and only the intercept shifts; `a/b = [π_s/(1 − π_s)]/[π/(1 − π)]`** — complete (Bayes' rule with the selection mechanism; hypotheses: sampling independent of `x` given `y`); counterexample when selection depends on `x` (computed); (g) **stratified bootstrap validity: resampling `m` positives and `n_0` negatives separately with replacement mimics the sampling design in which class counts are fixed; the variance of recall and FPR estimates then equals `R(1 − R)/m` and `F(1 − F)/n_0` in the plain-sample limit; the plain bootstrap has random class counts and yields a resample with zero positives with probability exactly `(1 − m/n)^n ≈ e^{−m}` (derive; e.g., `m = 5`: `0.0067`)** — complete; **counterexamples**: **PPV `0.083` for an excellent-looking detector at `π = 0.001` (computed)**; **AUC unchanged while precision triples when negatives are subsampled by 3× (computed)**; **a 10-positive recall interval of roughly 0.60 to 0.98 for `R̂ = 0.9` (Wilson, computed)**; **a case–control model's probabilities read as deployment risks (seeded)**; **a CV fold with zero positives (computed probability)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal prevalence/PPV calculators, Clopper–Pearson/Wilson intervals, stratified bootstrap, case–control correction, and positives-per-fold calculators vs SciPy and exact enumeration**; **tool card** for `ratelab.frame` (prevalence, PPV/NPV and odds calculators; sampling-design simulators (cohort, stratified, case–control) with known fractions; prior-shift correction; interval methods on the right denominators; stratified bootstrap; positives-per-fold and minimum-fold planners; baseline cost calculators; rare-event-card validator; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).
- **L4:** **rare-event framing in production (an MLOps lens)**: **the event definition and label horizon as versioned artifacts; label-delay-aware training windows and evaluation (only cases old enough to be labelled; a "mature cohort" rule); prevalence monitoring (a drift in `π` changes precision and alert volume even if the model is unchanged: link Unit 6 and 5D `12-Data-Model-and-Service-Monitoring`); sampling-design documentation (every training set records its sampling fractions); positive-count budgets (a pre-registered minimum `m` per evaluation split below which the study reports "insufficient positives" instead of a number); handling of the *do-nothing* baseline in business reviews**; hostile inputs (zero positives; one positive; all positives; prevalence `> 0.5`; duplicate positives; positives concentrated in one entity; label leakage through post-event features; a sampling fraction of 0; `NaN` labels); **exhaustive verification** of the PPV formula **over all small `(R, F, π)` on a rational grid (exact `Fraction`)**, of AUC's prevalence invariance **over all small score/label tables (≤ 8 rows) with subsampled negatives**, of the rule-of-three bound **against exact binomial computation**, of the case–control correction **by exact enumeration over a small discrete population**, and of the zero-positive resample probability **over small `(n, m)` by enumeration** (domains named and sized; `EVIDENCE ONLY`); mutation lab (accuracy reported; PPV computed with the wrong denominator; recall interval using `n`; case–control intercept uncorrected or with the wrong sign; folds without a positives guard; plain bootstrap used with `m < 20`; PR-AUC compared across prevalences without `π`; labels from outside the horizon) with no surviving mutants; case study: a "92% recall" claim from eleven positives, and a case–control model deployed with uncorrected probabilities (original synthetic reproductions with `m`-aware intervals and a corrected intercept as fixes).

## Unit 2 — Class weights and cost-sensitive learning

- **L1:** **instead of changing the data, tell the learner that mistakes on the rare class cost more: give each rare-class example a bigger *weight* in the loss (and each common-class example a smaller one)**; **what a weight does, by hand: the weighted log loss on a four-row table; one rare-class row with weight 10 behaves like ten copies of it in the loss, without duplicating data**; **cost-sensitive learning in plain words: if a missed event costs `c_FN` and a false alarm `c_FP`, a model that minimises *expected cost* should use a decision threshold `t = c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN)` on a *calibrated* probability (recalled from 5A-12 Unit 6) — or, equivalently in the right sense (L2–L3), be trained with weights proportional to the costs and then thresholded at 0.5**; **`class_weight='balanced'` in scikit-learn gives each class the weight `n/(K·n_k)`; what it does to a model with 1% positives (weights ≈ 50 and 0.5)**; **the important warning: after weighting, the model's output probabilities no longer estimate the *real* probability of the event — they are inflated toward the rare class; use them for ranking, or correct them (L2)**; **a first worked example by hand: a one-parameter logistic fit on a six-row imbalanced table with and without class weights: coefficients, predicted probabilities, and the cost at threshold 0.5 under a stated cost matrix**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **weights and costs in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the cost matrix and expected cost** (cost of each outcome; benefits as negative costs; normalising by the do-nothing cost; example-dependent costs (amount at risk) as per-row weights `c_FN(x)`; cost sources and uncertainty: `[ASSUMPTION]`, with a sensitivity analysis over a cost-ratio grid as a *required* step); **(2) weighted empirical risk** (`Σ w_i ℓ(y_i, f(x_i))`; `sample_weight` and `class_weight` in scikit-learn estimators and how each estimator treats them `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`; boosting `scale_pos_weight`-type parameters named; regularisation interacts with weights: effective `C` per class; normalising weights so the regularisation strength is comparable: a measured effect); **(3) what weighting does to the learned probabilities** (the weighted model targets the *tilted* posterior `p_w(x) = w₁p(x)/(w₁p(x) + w₀(1 − p(x)))` (derived in L3), so its probabilities are miscalibrated for the deployment prior but **rank-equivalent** to the true posterior (a monotone transform); the correction `p(x) = w₀p_w/(w₀p_w + w₁(1 − p_w))`; a worked check with a well-specified logistic model: weighting shifts the intercept by `ln(w₁/w₀)` and leaves the slopes unchanged (proved in L3); **for misspecified or flexible models weighting changes *where* the model spends capacity, not only the intercept: measured on a planted generator**); **(4) weights versus threshold moving** (for a *well-specified, correctly calibrated* model they give the same decisions (L3); for finite-capacity models they differ: weights change the fit, thresholds only the decision; a seeded comparison on planted-truth generators (cost regret vs the oracle threshold, with intervals) — **evidence about the generators only, no ranking**; the practical hybrid: weights to shape the fit, then calibrate and threshold on validation data); **(5) tree and boosting models** (class weights in split criteria and leaf values; `min_weight_fraction_leaf`; boosting with weights; weighted bagging and weighted forests `[VERSION-SENSITIVE]`; the effect on probability estimates (trees are poorly calibrated even unweighted: `../04-…` preview)); **(6) SVMs and margins** (class-weighted soft-margin SVM: the box constraint `C_k = C·w_k`; scores are not probabilities; a worked tiny example); **(7) multi-class and multi-label extensions** (a `K × K` cost matrix; one-vs-rest weights; the Bayes decision `argmin_j Σ_i p_i L_{ij}`; per-label thresholds; named and implemented for `K = 3`); **(8) cost curves and sensitivity** (expected cost across cost ratios and prevalences; the cost curve of a classifier vs the trivial classifiers (derived in L3: the normalised expected cost as a function of the *probability-cost function* `PC(+) = πc_FN/(πc_FN + (1 − π)c_FP)`); robustness of the chosen operating point to cost misspecification: a table); **(9) when costs are unknown** (using a plausible range and reporting the set of operating points that are optimal for some cost in the range (the lower envelope of the cost curve); `JUDGEMENT`); **(10) reporting** (weights or costs used; the correction applied to probabilities; cost curves; sensitivity)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **Bayes decision with costs (recalled from 5A-12 Unit 6): with calibrated `p(x)`, predict positive iff `p(x)·c_FN > (1 − p(x))·c_FP`, i.e., `p(x) > c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN)`; the minimum expected cost is `E[min(p c_FN, (1 − p)c_FP)]`** — recalled with proof; (b) **weighting tilts the posterior: minimising the population weighted risk with weights `w₁` on positives and `w₀` on negatives is equivalent to minimising the unweighted risk under the reweighted distribution with prior `π_w = w₁π/(w₁π + w₀(1 − π))` and the *same* class-conditional distributions; hence the Bayes-optimal scorer of the weighted problem is `p_w(x) = w₁p(x)/(w₁p(x) + w₀(1 − p(x)))`, i.e., odds multiplied by `w₁/w₀`** — complete (the change-of-measure argument); (c) **cost-proportional weights make `0.5` cost-optimal: choose `w₁/w₀ = c_FN/c_FP`; the cost-optimal threshold `t = c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN)` on `p` maps to `t_w = w₁t/(w₁t + w₀(1 − t)) = 1/2` on `p_w`** — complete (algebra); (d) **logistic regression with weights: for a *well-specified* model `logit p(x) = β₀ + βᵀx`, the weighted population optimum has `logit p_w(x) = β₀ + ln(w₁/w₀) + βᵀx` — only the intercept moves; for a misspecified model the slopes move too (a computed two-point example)** — complete for the well-specified case; counterexample for misspecified; (e) **`class_weight='balanced'`: with `n` samples, `K` classes, and `n_k` in class `k`, `w_k = n/(K n_k)`; the weighted class totals are all equal to `n/K`; for binary data with `m` positives, `w₁ = n/(2m)`, `w₀ = n/(2(n − m))`** — complete (arithmetic; verify against `compute_class_weight` for the installed version); (f) **the normalised expected cost: with `FNR` and `FPR` of a classifier and `PC(+) = πc_FN/(πc_FN + (1 − π)c_FP)`, the expected cost divided by the maximum possible expected cost `πc_FN + (1 − π)c_FP` equals `FNR·PC(+) + FPR·(1 − PC(+))`; a line in `PC(+)`; the always-negative classifier is the line `PC(+)`; always-positive is `1 − PC(+)`; the lower envelope over thresholds is the classifier's cost curve** — complete (algebra); (g) **weights and regularisation: for ridge-regularised weighted least squares with total weight rescaled by `c`, the solution equals the unweighted-scale solution with the penalty rescaled by `1/c`; so the same `λ` means different things under different weight normalisations** — complete; **counterexamples**: **weighted-model probabilities read as risks (computed: 0.50 vs the true 0.02)**; **weights matching threshold moving for a well-specified model and not for a misspecified one (seeded; measured regret with interval)**; **the same `C` giving different decision boundaries under two weight normalisations (computed)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal weighted logistic regression (Newton/IRLS), weighted SVM dual box constraints for tiny data, probability correction, cost-curve calculators vs scikit-learn and SciPy (agreement conditions: penalty scaling, intercept handling)**; **tool card** for `ratelab.cost` (cost matrices with sensitivity grids; weighted losses and learners (logistic, tree-stump demonstrations); weight builders (balanced, cost-proportional, example-dependent); prior-shift probability correction; cost curves and lower envelopes; weights-vs-threshold comparison harness with planted truth; multi-class Bayes decisions; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).
- **L4:** **cost-sensitive learning in production (an MLOps lens)**: **cost matrices as governed artifacts (owner, source, date, uncertainty range; changes are model changes); shipping both the *ranking score* and, where probabilities are consumed, the *corrected* probability with its calibration check (link `../04-…`); documenting which weights were used so that probabilities are interpretable downstream; cost-drift (when the cost of a miss changes the threshold moves, not necessarily the model); sensitivity reports in the model card; consumer contracts (a downstream system that treats a weighted score as a probability)**; hostile inputs (zero or negative costs; `c_FN ≤ c_FP`; weights of zero; extreme weight ratios causing unstable fits; missing classes; example-dependent costs of `NaN`; a model with all-equal scores); **exhaustive verification** of the posterior-tilt formula **over all small discrete populations (exact `Fraction`)**, of the cost-proportional-weights identity **over a grid of `(w₁, w₀, t)`**, of the cost-curve identity **over a grid of `(π, c_FN, c_FP, FNR, FPR)`**, and of the balanced-weight formula **over all small class counts** (domains named and sized; `EVIDENCE ONLY`); mutation lab (weights applied to the test set; probabilities from a weighted model reported uncorrected; `c_FN` and `c_FP` swapped; ratio inverted; balanced weights computed on the full data before the split; regularisation not rescaled; cost sensitivity omitted) with no surviving mutants; case study: a weighted model whose 0.5-threshold alerts were "calibrated risks" in a dashboard, and a cost-ratio change that required a threshold change but triggered a pointless retrain (original synthetic reproductions with probability correction and cost-sensitivity tables as fixes).

## Unit 3 — Under- and oversampling, and SMOTE's limitations

- **L1:** **resampling changes the *data* instead of the loss: *undersampling* throws away common-class rows; *oversampling* repeats rare-class rows; *SMOTE* creates new, synthetic rare-class rows between existing ones**; **how SMOTE works, in plain words: pick a rare-class row, pick one of its `k` nearest rare-class neighbours, and create a new point somewhere on the line segment between them**; **what resampling does and does not do: it changes how much weight the learner gives each class (like class weights); it does **not** add real information — synthetic rows are made from the rows you already have**; **a first worked example by hand: a 2-D toy set with 3 rare-class points; the nearest-neighbour pairs; two SMOTE points computed with stated random draws; and a counterexample where the new points fall in the middle of the common class**; **three rules that will recur: resample only the *training* data; never resample validation or test data; and remember that resampled models output probabilities for a *resampled* world (Unit 2's warning, again)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **resampling in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) random oversampling and undersampling** (duplicate or drop rows to reach a target ratio; sampling with and without replacement; the equivalence of integer oversampling and weighting for the *loss* (recalled from 5A-12 Unit 6) and the differences for *algorithms that depend on counts or neighbourhoods* (trees' leaf purity and `min_samples_leaf`, `k`-NN, bootstrap-based ensembles); **duplicates cause memorisation**: a seeded measurement with `k`-NN and deep trees: training score inflates, validation score does not improve; undersampling's information loss: the variance of the fitted model as a function of the number of retained negatives (measured and derived for a mean-difference classifier)); **(2) SMOTE and variants** (the algorithm with `k` neighbours and uniform interpolation `x_new = x_i + u(x_nn − x_i)`, `u ~ U(0, 1)`; **Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN, SMOTE-NC (mixed numeric/categorical), and cluster-based variants named `CITED [VERIFY]`**; the learner's from-scratch SMOTE (numeric features) compared with `imbalanced-learn`'s only if installed (agreement conditions: neighbour search, random stream, `k`); scaling is required before neighbour search (5A-03); the effect of `k` and of the target ratio: measured); **(3) the limitations, each demonstrated** (**(a)** synthetic points are interpolations, so they stay in the **convex hull** of the minority sample: no extrapolation, no new modes (proved in L3); **(b)** they can fall **inside the majority region** when the minority is multi-modal or overlapping — creating label noise (a computed example and a measured rate of "synthetic points whose true posterior favours the majority" on a planted-truth generator); **(c)** they **ignore categorical and constrained structure** (one-hot interpolation gives fractional values; integer/range/unit constraints violated: a worked example; SMOTE-NC as a partial fix); **(d)** they **amplify noise and outliers** among minority points; ## Unit 4 — Resampling inside training folds

- **L1:** **the number-one resampling mistake: resample first, then split (or cross-validate); some copies — or the parents of some synthetic rows — end up in the validation fold, so the validation fold contains rows the model has effectively already seen; the score looks great and the deployment score is not**; **the rule, in plain words: *split first; resample only the training part of each split; evaluate on untouched validation rows with the real class distribution***; **why it is a leak: an oversampled rare row has several identical copies; if one copy is in training and another in validation, the model is graded on a row it memorised; SMOTE rows built from validation-fold parents carry validation information into training**; **a first worked example by hand: 4 rare rows duplicated 3× (12 rows), split into 3 folds at random: the number of validation rows that have a twin in training (counted for a stated assignment); then the correct procedure: split 4 rare rows first, duplicate inside each training part, and count again (zero)**; **how to do it with scikit-learn: a standard `Pipeline` cannot resample (its steps transform `X` only), so the learner writes a small fold-safe wrapper (and names `imbalanced-learn`'s pipeline as the library option)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
