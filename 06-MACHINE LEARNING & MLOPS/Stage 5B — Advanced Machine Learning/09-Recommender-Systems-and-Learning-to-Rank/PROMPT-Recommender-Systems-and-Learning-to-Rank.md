## Unit 1 — Recommendation problems and baselines

- **L1:** **a recommender answers "which items should this user see next?" It may produce a ranked list on a page, a "similar items" shelf, or a short candidate list for a later ranking step. ## Unit 2 — User–item matrices and feedback types

- **L1:** **the core data object is the user–item matrix: rows are users, columns are items, and an entry holds a rating or an event count — almost all entries are *missing* (unobserved), not zero. *Explicit* feedback (star ratings) says how much a user liked an item; *implicit* feedback (views, clicks, purchases, watch time) says only that something happened, and *no event does not mean dislike* — the user may never have seen the item**; **a first worked example by hand: a four-user, five-item rating table with eight observed entries: the density, the average ratings per user and per item, the row/column sums (their double-counting invariant `Σ_u|Ω_u| = Σ_i|Ω_i| = nnz`), and how binarising "rating ≥ 4" changes the relevant set (computed exactly); then the observed mean of ratings when happier users rate more often (`4.06` against a true `3.0`)**; **sparse storage in plain words: store only the observed entries**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

(b) **MNAR bias of the observed mean: if a rating of value `r` is observed with probability `π(r)` independently of the user given `r`, the expected observed mean is `Σ_r rP(r)π(r)/Σ_rP(r)π(r)`; for a uniform true distribution on `{1,…,5}` and `π = (0.1, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8)` it is `6.5/1.6 = 4.0625` versus the true mean `3`** — complete (computed); ## Unit 3 — Collaborative filtering

- **L1:** **collaborative filtering recommends by *what similar users (or similar items) did*: *user-based* — find users whose histories resemble yours and recommend what they liked; *item-based* — find items that are liked by the same people as the items you liked**; **similarity measures in plain words: *cosine* (the angle between two vectors), *Pearson* (cosine after subtracting means), *Jaccard* (overlap divided by union, for sets)**; **a first worked example by hand: the four-user, five-item table: the cosine and Jaccard similarity of two items, an item-based score for one user as the sum of similarities to their items, and the top-2 recommendations (computed exactly); then two items that share one single fan and look "identical" (cosine 1)**; **why neighbourhood methods are easy to explain ("because you watched X") and easy to fool on sparse data**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **neighbourhood collaborative filtering in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the two families** (user-user and item-item; why item-item is preferred when items are fewer or more stable than users (a measured comparison of cost and quality on generators: evidence only); ## Unit 4 — Matrix factorisation

- **L1:** **matrix factorisation explains the sparse user–item matrix with a small number `k` of hidden "taste" dimensions: each user gets a vector `p_u` (how much they like each dimension) and each item a vector `q_i` (how much it has of each dimension), and the predicted preference is their dot product `p_u · q_i`. The vectors are *learned* so that the predictions match the observed entries, with a penalty (regularisation) that keeps them small**; **bias terms capture "this user rates high" and "this item is liked by everyone"**; **a first worked example by hand: a rank-1 `2 × 2` matrix `[[1, 2], [2, 4]] = p qᵀ` with `p = q = (1, 2)ᵀ`, one exact alternating update of `p` given `q` (the formula `p_u = Σ q_i r_ui/(Σ q_i² + λ)`), and the effect of `λ = 0` versus `λ = 1` on the fit (computed exactly)**; **the key cautions: a lower rating-prediction error does not guarantee a better ranking; too many factors with too little data memorise the training entries**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **factorisation in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the model** (`r̂_ui = μ + b_u + b_i + p_uᵀq_i`; the regularised squared-error objective `Σ_{(u,i)∈Ω}(r_ui − r̂_ui)² + λ(‖p_u‖² + ‖q_i‖² + b_u² + b_i²)`; **why missing entries are not zeros** (a truncated SVD of the zero-filled matrix predicts zeros for unobserved pairs: measured on a generator); relation to the SVD (Eckart–Young for fully observed matrices, recalled from 4B/5B-05) and why it does not apply with missing entries); **(2) optimisation** (**ALS**: alternate exact ridge solutions for all `p_u` then all `q_i` (L3); each sweep costs `O(nnz·k² + (m + n)k³)`; **SGD** with learning rate, per-example updates `p_u ← p_u + η(e q_i − λp_u)`, `q_i ← q_i + η(e p_u − λq_i)`, cost `O(nnz·k)` per epoch; initialisation scale and seeds; convergence monitoring on a *validation* block, early stopping; the from-scratch implementations compared with each other and with `TruncatedSVD`/`NMF` only on fully observed toy matrices); **(3) implicit feedback** (the weighted objective `Σ_{u,i}c_ui(p_ui − p_uᵀq_i)² + λ(…)` over *all* pairs with preference `p_ui = 1{r_ui > 0}` and confidence `c_ui = 1 + αr_ui`; the efficient ALS identity (L3) that avoids touching all `m·n` pairs; `α` and `λ` as hyperparameters; **named: BPR as the pairwise alternative (Unit 8)**); **(4) choosing `k`, `λ`, and regularisation** (the number of parameters `(m + n)k` versus `nnz` (`m = 10^5`, `n = 10^4`, `k = 64` gives `7.04 × 10^6 > 10^6`): overfitting regime; time-ordered validation of `k` and `λ` with a counted grid; measured sensitivity of ranking metrics to `k`; no recommended `k`); **(5) identifiability and the geometry of factors** (`P → PA`, `Q → QA^{−T}` leaves predictions unchanged (L3); with the `ℓ₂` penalty only orthogonal `A` preserves the objective, so factors are unique up to rotation; consequences: embeddings from different runs are compared only after alignment (Procrustes: named; link `../05-…`); individual factors have no intrinsic meaning: interpretability claims about "genre dimensions" are `HEURISTIC` and measured against the planted factors); **(6) evaluation** (RMSE on held-out ratings versus ranking metrics at `K` (they can disagree: Unit 8's counterexample); full-catalogue scoring cost `m·n·k` guarded; seen-item filtering; time-ordered splits; the popularity baseline row; oracle gap on planted low-rank truth with intervals — evidence only); **(7) variants named** (non-negative factorisation (`NMF`), SVD++ and neighbour-augmented models, factorisation machines, time-aware factors, tensor models): named with one-line purposes, never required; **(8) side information and cold start** (feature-augmented factors: Unit 5; folding-in for new users: Unit 7); **(9) serving** (precompute user and item factors; top-`K` by exact scoring or approximate search (Unit 6); incremental updates by folding-in; artifact export); **(10) reporting** (objective, solver, `k`, `λ`, `α`, initialisation, convergence criterion, validation design, ranking metrics with intervals, popularity baseline, cost counts)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **ALS user update: with item factors fixed, minimising `Σ_{i∈Ω_u}(r_ui − p_uᵀq_i)² + λ‖p_u‖²` over `p_u` is a ridge regression with the unique minimiser `p_u = (Q_uᵀQ_u + λI)^{-1}Q_uᵀr_u`, where `Q_u` stacks the rows `q_iᵀ`, `i ∈ Ω_u` (set the gradient to zero; the Hessian `2(Q_uᵀQ_u + λI)` is positive definite for `λ > 0`)** — complete; (b) **ALS never increases the objective: each user step minimises the objective exactly over `p_u` with all else fixed, and the objective is a sum over users of terms involving a single `p_u` (plus terms not involving `P`), so the joint `P`-update decreases (or keeps) it; likewise for `Q`; the objective is bounded below by `0`, so it converges (to a stationary point, not necessarily a global minimum: the problem is non-convex jointly)** — complete; (c) **non-identifiability: for any invertible `A`, `(PA)(QA^{−T})ᵀ = PAA^{−1}Qᵀ = PQᵀ`; (d) **unregularised overfitting: if a user has `|Ω_u| ≤ k` observed items whose factor vectors are linearly independent, the system `Q_up_u = r_u` is solvable exactly, so the training error for that user is `0` for *any* ratings, and the held-out predictions are unconstrained; `λ > 0` makes the solution unique and shrinks it** — complete; (e) **the rank-1 example: `R = [[1, 2], [2, 4]]` equals `pqᵀ` for `p = q = (1, 2)ᵀ`; one ALS user-step with item factors `q = (1, 2)` and `λ = 0` returns `p_u = r_u·q/‖q‖²` (`= 1` for `r_u = (1, 2)`; (g) **implicit ALS efficiency: with `C_u = diag(c_ui)` and `C_u − I` nonzero only on `Ω_u`, `YᵀC_uY = YᵀY + Yᵀ(C_u − I)Y`, and the second term is a sum over `i ∈ Ω_u` of `(c_ui − 1)q_iq_iᵀ`; the Gram matrix `YᵀY` is computed once per sweep (`O(nk²)`), each user then costs `O(|Ω_u|k² + k³)`; for `m = 10^5`, `n = 10^4`, `k = 32`, `nnz = 10^6`: about `10^9 + 10^7` operations against `m·n·k² = 10^{12}` naively** — complete (identity and arithmetic); (h) **the zero-filled SVD predicts zeros: for the zero-filled matrix `R₀`, the best rank-`k` approximation (Eckart–Young) minimises the error over *all* `m·n` entries including the unobserved ones, which it is therefore trying to reproduce as zeros; a computed `3 × 3` example** — complete; **counterexamples**: **interpolation of training ratings with `k ≥ |Ω_u|` and zero test accuracy (seeded)**; **embeddings from two runs related by a rotation yet unaligned (computed)**; **a zero-filled SVD recommender ranking "unseen" items by how popular they are (seeded)**; **a lower-RMSE model with a lower NDCG (seeded, Unit 8)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal ALS (explicit and implicit) and SGD vs closed-form ridge solves, `scipy.sparse.linalg.svds`, `TruncatedSVD`, and `NMF` on fully observed toys (agreement conditions: centring, regularisation scaling, initialisation)**; **tool card** for `reclab.mf` (biased MF with ALS and SGD, explicit and implicit; objective monitors and early stopping on a validation block; rank/`λ` grids with fit counters and budget guards; folding-in; Procrustes alignment; planted low-rank generators with true factors; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).

## Unit 5 — Content-based and hybrid recommendation

- **L1:** **a *content-based* recommender uses descriptions of the items — text, genres, categories, prices — to recommend items similar to those a user liked. Build a profile for the user from the items they interacted with (for example, the average of their item vectors), then rank candidate items by similarity to the profile. A *hybrid* recommender combines content with collaborative signals**; **a first worked example by hand: five items described by TF-IDF-style vectors over three topics: the user profile as the mean of two liked item vectors, the cosine of each candidate with the profile, and the top-2 list (computed exactly); then a candidate from a topic the user has never seen, which scores 0 by construction**; **why hybrids: content helps new items that nobody has interacted with yet; collaborative signals capture taste that item descriptions do not**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 6 — Two-tower retrieval and candidate generation

- **L1:** **catalogues are too large to score every item with an expensive model for every user, so a fast first stage — *candidate generation* — picks a few hundred promising items. - **L1:** **ranking asks for the right *order* of items, not for accurate scores. ## Unit 9 — Precision/recall at K, MAP, and NDCG

- **L1:** **a ranking metric scores a *list*, looking only at the top `K` positions a user will actually see. *Precision@K*: what share of the top `K` is relevant; *Recall@K*: what share of all relevant items made the top `K`; *Hit rate@K*: did at least one relevant item appear; *MAP*: the average precision, rewarding relevant items near the top; *NDCG*: a graded-relevance score that discounts lower positions and is divided by the best possible score for that user**; **a first worked example by hand: a six-item list with relevance grades `(3, 2, 3, 0, 1, 2)`: `DCG = 13.848`, the ideal ordering `(3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 0)` has `IDCG = 14.595`, so `NDCG = 0.949` (gain `2^{rel} − 1`, discount `1/log₂(1 + position)`); and a binary list `(1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1)` with three relevant items: `AP = (1/1 + 2/3 + 3/6)/3 = 0.722`, `P@3 = 0.667` (computed exactly)**; **average over *users*, and say which users were included**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 10 — Offline versus online evaluation

- **L1:** **offline evaluation replays *logged* data: it asks how well a model would have predicted what users did. Online evaluation (an *A/B test*) shows the new recommender to real users and measures what happens. They can disagree, for a basic reason: the log only contains what the *old* system chose to show, so it says little about items the old system never showed**; **a first worked example by hand: two items with true click rates `0.3` and `0.6`; the old system shows item 1 with probability `0.8` and item 2 with `0.2`; a new system would show item 2 with probability `0.8`. The average click rate in the log is `0.36` (the *old* system's performance); the true value of the new system is `0.54`; *inverse propensity scoring* recovers `0.54` by weighting each logged event by `π_new/π_old` (`0.25` for item 1, `4` for item 2) (computed exactly)**; **online tests measure real behaviour but cost time, traffic, and risk — so offline checks narrow the candidates and online tests decide**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

**interference** (users and items interact: marketplace and network effects: named); **position bias** (Unit 8)); **(2) the logged-bandit view** (context `x` (user, time), action `a` (shown item or slate), reward `r` (click/purchase), **logging policy** `π₀(a | x)` (the *propensity* of what was shown), **target policy** `π`; ## Unit 11 — Popularity bias, diversity, and feedback loops

- **L1:** **recommenders tend to favour already-popular items — the data contain more interactions for them — and what they recommend becomes the next round of data: a *feedback loop*. Left alone, exposure concentrates on a few items, the catalogue's long tail starves, and lists become repetitive. We measure this with *coverage* (how many distinct items get recommended), *exposure concentration* (a Gini coefficient), *average popularity of recommended items*, and *diversity within a list***; **a first worked example by hand: four items; a popularity-only recommender shows only item 4 to everyone: exposure `(0, 0, 0, 10)`: coverage `1/4`, Gini `0.75` (the maximum for four items); then a greedy re-ranker (*MMR*) that, after picking item A, prefers C (similar to A: `0.1`) over B (very similar to A: `0.95`) even though B is more relevant (computed exactly)**; **accuracy is not the only goal: a slightly less accurate but more varied list can serve users and the catalogue better — a trade-off to measure, not to assume**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **bias, diversity, and loops in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) popularity bias** (the long-tail item distribution (Zipf-like exponent as a generator parameter); *popularity bias in recommendations* = recommended items are more popular than the users' own histories: **measured by the average popularity of recommended items versus of the user's items, and by the share of recommendations from the head**; sources: skewed training data, the objective (accuracy rewards popular items), neighbourhood/co-occurrence statistics (Unit 3), in-batch negatives (Unit 6), exposure bias (Unit 10); **a popularity-bias-amplification statistic with its own uncertainty**); **(2) catalogue-level measures** (item coverage `|{recommended items}|/n`; the **Gini coefficient of exposure**, `G = (2Σ_ii·x_{(i)})/(nΣx) − (n + 1)/n` for ascending-sorted exposures `x_{(i)}` (L3); the Lorenz curve as a table; long-tail share; **entropy of exposure**; each reported with its definition and the generator's truth); **(3) list-level measures** (**intra-list diversity** `ILD = (1/(K(K − 1)))Σ_{i≠j}(1 − sim(i, j))` with the similarity from content or embeddings (state which); **novelty** (self-information `−log₂ popularity`: named); **serendipity** (unexpected but relevant: definitions vary: named, `HEURISTIC`); **calibration of recommendations** (the distribution of categories in the list versus in the user's history, compared with a smoothed KL divergence: named); **the accuracy–diversity trade-off curve**); **(4) re-ranking for diversity** (**MMR**: greedily pick `argmax_{i∉S}[λ·rel(i) − (1 − λ)·max_{j∈S}sim(i, j)]`, reducing to relevance ranking at `λ = 1` (L3); **(6) other bias channels** (position bias (Unit 8); conformity and herding (named); popularity of *producers*; **consumer-side effects on real users (filter bubbles, polarisation, wellbeing) are empirical social-science questions: the module makes no claims, names the literature `[VERIFY]`, and models only the planted loop**); **(7) fairness of exposure (awareness only)** (exposure share by item/provider group versus catalogue or merit share; group definitions and small-cell suppression; the difficulty of "merit"; trade-offs with accuracy;
