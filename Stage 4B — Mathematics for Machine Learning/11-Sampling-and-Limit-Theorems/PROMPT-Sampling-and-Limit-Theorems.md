## Unit 2 — The law of large numbers

- **L1:** **the idea: averages of many independent observations settle down to the expected value** — **the relative frequency of an event converges to its probability; the sample mean converges to the population mean** (**what it does *not* say: it does not say the *count* of heads minus `n/2` shrinks (it grows like `√n`), and it says nothing about the *next* flip — the "law of averages"/gambler's fallacy, treated explicitly**); **a picture: running means of fair-coin flips, die rolls, and uniform draws (seeded, several paths), approaching the mean, with the `±` standard-error band shrinking like `1/√n` (S4B-09)**; **running mean vs running sum, side by side**; **what "converges" means informally: for any tolerance `ε`, the probability that the mean is farther than `ε` from `μ` tends to `0` as `n` grows — the weak law (the formal definition in L2)**; **Monte Carlo estimation as an application**: **estimate `P(A)` or `E[g(X)]` by an average; the S4B-09 standard error `σ/√n`; a first example with an exact answer**; **a worked comparison of the *exact* probability that the sample mean of `n` fair-coin flips is within `0.05` of `0.5` (exact binomial sums, `Fraction`/`Decimal`) with the Chebyshev bound from S4B-09 — the bound holds and is loose**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **precise statements**: **the weak law: for i.i.d. `Xᵢ` with finite mean `μ`, `X̄ₙ →ᴾ μ`, i.e., `P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) → 0` for every `ε > 0` (the general statement is Khinchin's, `CITED [VERIFY]`; the finite-variance case is proved in L3)**; **the strong law: with probability one, the *sequence* of running means converges to `μ` (statement; `CITED [VERIFY]`; the difference from the weak law explained by the picture of a single path vs the probability at each `n`; proved in `4C-04`)**; **convergence in probability defined (`Yₙ →ᴾ c`)**; **what the laws need**: **independence (or weak dependence), identical distribution, a finite mean — and the failure modes shown**: **the Cauchy-shaped density (no mean): the running mean never settles, and the mean of `n` i.i.d. Cauchy draws has the *same* distribution as a single draw (stated `CITED [VERIFY]` and verified exactly for `n = 2` by the S4B-06 convolution integral and by quadrature, and by simulation across `n`)**; **a power law with `1 < α ≤ 2` (finite mean, infinite variance): the mean converges, but slowly and erratically — the S4B-06/10 densities, measured**; **a power law with `α ≤ 1` (infinite mean): the running mean grows**; **dependent data**: **strongly dependent or non-stationary data (a trend, a regime change) may not average to the population value; positively correlated data average more slowly (the effective sample size `n/(1 + (n − 1)ρ)` for equicorrelated variables — derived in Unit 4); an AR(1) series' mean (measured)**; **the law for functions of data: `(1/n)Σ g(Xᵢ) →ᴾ E[g(X)]` (applying the law to `g(Xᵢ)`) — so the empirical distribution function converges pointwise (`F̂ₙ(x) →ᴾ F(x)`, the law applied to the indicators), the sample variance is consistent (`s² →ᴾ σ²`, from the law applied to `Xᵢ²` and `X̄ₙ`), the Monte Carlo estimator is consistent**; **uniform convergence named**: **the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem (`sup|F̂ₙ − F| → 0`, `CITED [VERIFY]`) and the DKW inequality `P(sup|F̂ₙ − F| > ε) ≤ 2e^{−2nε²}` (`CITED [VERIFY]`; Massart's constant; a seeded numerical check of the bound — the S4B-09 Unit 6 deferral closed)**; **the law and empirical risk — the most important ML caution**: **for a *fixed* function `h` (a fixed model), the average loss on `n` i.i.d. fresh examples converges to the expected loss; but when the model is *chosen using the same data*, the training average is optimistically biased — the law applies to a fixed function, not to a data-dependent one (uniform laws named, `4C`; VC theory named, no claim)**; **selection bias made exact**: **the expected maximum of the test accuracies of `m` equally-good models evaluated on the *same* test set exceeds the common true accuracy — computed *exactly* for small `(m, n)` by enumerating the joint outcomes of independent binomial counts (`Fraction`), and for larger values by the exact CDF of the maximum `(F(k))ᵐ` (S4B-09) — the "winner's curse" quantified; the cure (a fresh held-out set for the winner) shown exactly**; **tail bounds as the engineering form of the law: Chebyshev (S4B-09), the exponential-Markov (Chernoff) technique and Hoeffding's inequality for bounded variables (`P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ 2exp(−2nε²/(b − a)²)`, statement; proof in L3 or `CITED`, say which) — compared in a table with the *exact* binomial tail and the CLT-based approximation**; **sample-size calculators for evaluation, derived and compared (computed, not recalled): to estimate an accuracy within `ε` with failure probability `δ`: Chebyshev worst case `n ≥ 1/(4ε²δ)`, Hoeffding `n ≥ ln(2/δ)/(2ε²)`, CLT-based `n ≥ (z_{1−δ/2}/(2ε))²` at the worst-case `p = ½`, and the *exact* smallest `n` by binomial summation — a table over `(ε, δ)` with the exact column as the oracle; the observation that Chebyshev is far more conservative and that the CLT can under- or over-state for small `n` or skewed `p`**; **the cost of precision: halving the error needs four times the data; diminishing returns (arithmetic)**; **averaging in high dimension (S4B-04): the norm of the mean of `n` i.i.d. vectors converges to the mean vector; the law applied coordinate-wise (S4B-01 norms)**; **a mini-batch average as a sample mean (S4B-08): the batch gradient converges to the full gradient as `B → N` and its error shrinks like `1/√B` — restated with the law**.

## Unit 4 — Sampling distributions and standard error

- **L1:** **a statistic computed from a sample (a mean, a proportion, a median, a variance, a test accuracy) is itself random: a different sample would have given a different value — its *sampling distribution* is the distribution of that statistic over repeated, independent samples from the population (the what-is-random rule)**; **the standard error (SE) of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution — the typical size of the sampling fluctuation**; **SD vs SE**: **the standard deviation describes how spread out the *data* are; the standard error describes how precisely the *statistic* estimates the target; the SE of the mean is `σ/√n` (S4B-09; derived there, now used and interpreted)**; **the sampling distribution built by enumeration on a tiny population**: **a population of five values; list *all* `5² = 25` samples of size `2` with replacement and *all* `C(5, 2) = 10` samples without replacement; compute the mean of each; tabulate the exact sampling distribution of `X̄`, its mean (equal to the population mean — unbiasedness) and its variance (`σ²/n` with replacement; `(σ²/n)·(N − n)/(N − 1)` without — the S4B-08/09 finite-population factor; exact `Fraction`)**; **the same by simulation: draw many samples, compute the statistic each time, and look at the histogram — the "repeated-experiment" picture, with `R` replications and its own Monte Carlo error**; **the SE of a proportion: `√(p(1 − p)/n)`** (S4B-10 Unit 1; the test-set accuracy's SE); **estimating the SE from one sample: `ŜE = s/√n` (plug-in; `s` with `ddof = 1`) and `√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n)`**; **the SE shrinks like `1/√n` (halve the error: quadruple the data; arithmetic)**; **reporting: `estimate ± SE` and the number of observations; not `± SD`**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact enumeration.
