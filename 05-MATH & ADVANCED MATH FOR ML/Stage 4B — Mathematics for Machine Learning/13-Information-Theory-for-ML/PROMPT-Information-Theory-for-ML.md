## Unit 1 — Information and surprise

- **L1:** **the idea: the less probable an event, the more surprising it is, and surprise should add for independent events**; **the surprisal (self-information) of an outcome with probability `p`: `I(x) = −log p(x)`** — **a certain event (`p = 1`) has surprisal `0`; halving the probability adds one bit; an event of probability `0` has infinite surprisal (the zero-probability rule)**; **units: bits (`log₂`), nats (`ln`), and hartleys (`log₁₀`); conversion `1 nat = 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.4427 bits` (computed, not recalled) and `1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.6931 nats`**; **worked examples by hand**: a fair coin (1 bit per flip), a fair die (`log₂ 6` bits), a biased coin (`p = 0.9`: heads `0.152` bits, tails `3.32` bits — computed to stated precision), a card drawn from a deck, a single letter from a skewed alphabet; **additivity: the surprisal of two independent events is the sum (`−log(pq) = −log p − log q`, 4A-06), so the surprisal of a sequence is the sum of per-symbol surprisals (the S4B-09 chain rule: `−log P(x₁…x_T) = Σ −log P(x_t | x_{<t})`)**; **"how many yes/no questions?": `log₂ N` bits identify one of `N` equally likely outcomes (a worked "twenty questions" example; the binary-search link, S3)**; **surprise vs probability in a plot (the `−log p` curve, steep near `0`)**; **the zero-probability warning: a model assigning probability `0` to something that happens has infinite surprisal — the loss is `+∞`**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact (dyadic) and high-precision (`Decimal`) checks.
- **L2:** **surprisal in ML**: **the per-example negative log-likelihood of a model is the surprisal of the observed label under the model's predicted distribution (the log loss, previewed in S4B-05; defined in Unit 3); the per-token loss of a sequence model is the surprisal of the next token given its context (an *original synthetic* example only — no claim about any real model)**; **anomaly scoring by surprisal: fit a simple model (S4B-10) to "normal" data and score new points by `−log p(x)`; thresholds (the S4B-12 error-rate view: the false-alarm rate at a threshold is a probability computed from the model; the S4B-10 Unit 5 caution about heavy tails and the 3-sigma rule); a seeded demonstration on synthetic data with the exact false-alarm rate under the fitted model**; **log-odds and weight of evidence: the log-likelihood ratio `log[P(E | H)/P(E | ¬H)]` is a *difference of surprisals* (the surprisal of the evidence under the alternative minus under the hypothesis) — in bits this is the "weight of evidence", and posterior log-odds = prior log-odds + weight of evidence (S4B-09 Unit 3; the S4B-09 spam-filter updates are sums of such terms)**; **pointwise mutual information (closing the S4B-04 Project 01 construction)**: **`PMI(x; y) = log [p(x, y)/(p(x)p(y))] = I(x) − I(x | y) = I(y) − I(y | x)` — how much knowing `y` reduces the surprise of `x`; `PMI = 0` iff `x` and `y` are independent (as events); positive when they co-occur more than independence predicts and negative when less; the bound `PMI(x; y) ≤ min(I(x), I(y))` and the unbounded negative range; the positive PMI `max(PMI, 0)` used in the S4B-04 word-vector construction and why (negative PMI estimates from sparse counts are unreliable); the *small-count inflation* of PMI for rare events (an exact example: two words each seen once and seen together once have a very large PMI from tiny evidence; smoothing (S4B-09/10) and count thresholds as remedies, `HEURISTIC`) — with the S4B-04 counts matrix as a worked, exact example**; **the zero-probability policy at work (the S4B-09 spam filter and S4B-10 Dirichlet smoothing, now as an *infinite surprise* that smoothing converts into a finite one; clipping `p ≥ ε` vs add-`α` smoothing — the two ways a cross-entropy loss stays finite, their different biases, a measured example)**; **units in practice: reporting a loss in nats vs bits; the framework default of nats `[VERIFY]`; per-token vs per-character vs per-sample vs summed; converting between them (Unit 5)**; **numerical reality: the smallest representable probability and the largest finite surprisal in float32 and float64 (derived from `np.finfo`; `−ln` of the smallest normal number), and why products of probabilities are never computed directly (S4B-09/10/11 reuse)**; **an introduction to coding (the interpretation of `−log₂ p` as an ideal code length): a prefix code assigns shorter codewords to likelier symbols; for dyadic probabilities the codeword lengths equal the surprisals exactly (a worked example with a 4-symbol distribution `(½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛)` and codes `0, 10, 110, 111`); the general statement is Unit 2**.
- **L3:** **proofs and derivations, written in full:** (a) **the uniqueness of the logarithmic form: if a function `f: (0, 1] → [0, ∞)` is continuous (or merely monotone non-increasing), satisfies `f(pq) = f(p) + f(q)`, and is not identically zero, then `f(p) = −c·log p` for some `c > 0` — a complete proof: from `f(pq) = f(p) + f(q)`, `f(pⁿ) = nf(p)`, so `f(p^{1/m}) = f(p)/m` and `f(p^{n/m}) = (n/m)f(p)` for all positive rationals; ## Unit 2 — Entropy

- **L1:** **the entropy of a distribution is the *average surprisal*: `H(X) = E[−log p(X)] = −Σₓ p(x) log p(x)`** — **the average number of bits needed to describe a draw (the coding reading), or equivalently how uncertain the outcome is**; **worked examples by hand**: a fair coin (1 bit), a biased coin (`p = 0.9`: `≈ 0.469` bits, computed), a fair `K`-sided die (`log₂ K`), a deterministic outcome (`0`), a dyadic distribution `(½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛)` (exactly `1.75` bits), a skewed three-outcome distribution; **the binary entropy function `h(p) = −p log₂ p − (1 − p) log₂(1 − p)` (a picture: zero at `p = 0` and `p = 1`, maximum `1` bit at `p = ½`, symmetric)**; **the three basic facts (proved in L3): `H ≥ 0`; `H = 0` iff the outcome is certain; `H ≤ log K` with equality iff the distribution is uniform**; **entropy vs variance: entropy depends only on the *probabilities*, not on the numeric values of the outcomes (two distributions with the same probabilities and wildly different values have the same entropy; the S4B-09 variance does not)**; **the "effective number of outcomes" `2^{H}` (a fair `K`-sided die has `K`; a skewed distribution has fewer) — closing the S4B-11 Project 01 "effective number of choices" definition**; **the convention `0 log 0 = 0` and why (proved in L3)**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact (dyadic) and `Decimal` checks.
- **L2:** **joint, conditional entropy, and mutual information (the pieces used in feature analysis and in the KL lesson)**: **`H(X, Y) = −Σ p(x, y) log p(x, y)`; the conditional entropy `H(Y | X) = Σₓ p(x) H(Y | X = x) = H(X, Y) − H(X)` (the chain rule `H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X)` — the entropy analogue of the S4B-09 chain rule of probability); independence ⇒ `H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)`; the mutual information `I(X; Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X, Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) = Σ p(x, y) log[p(x, y)/(p(x)p(y))]` (the *average PMI* — Unit 1) — the reduction in uncertainty about `Y` from knowing `X`; `I ≥ 0`, and `I = 0` iff `X` and `Y` are independent**; **worked exactly on joint tables (a 2×2 table; the S4B-09 confounder table; the XOR table: `X`, `Y` independent fair bits and `Z = X ⊕ Y` — pairwise independent but jointly determined: `I(X; Z) = 0` yet `I(X, Y; Z) = 1` bit, the entropy form of the S4B-09 "pairwise vs mutual independence" example)**; **mutual information detects what correlation cannot (closing S4B-09 Unit 8): `X` uniform on `{−1, 0, 1}` and `Y = X²` have correlation `0` and mutual information `I(X; Y) = H(Y) = h(⅓) ≈ 0.918` bits (computed exactly) — `Y` is a deterministic function of `X`**; **entropy of a function: `H(f(X)) ≤ H(X)` (merging outcomes cannot increase entropy; proved in L3)**; **entropy and coding (the source-coding view)**: **a prefix code (no codeword is a prefix of another) assigns each symbol a codeword of length `lᵢ`; Kraft's inequality `Σ 2^{−lᵢ} ≤ 1` is necessary and sufficient for the existence of such a code (the necessity proved in L3); the average length `L = Σ pᵢlᵢ` of any prefix code satisfies `L ≥ H` (proved in L3 from Gibbs' inequality, S4B-10 Unit 2); the Shannon code lengths `⌈−log₂ pᵢ⌉` give `H ≤ L < H + 1`; the Huffman algorithm (merge the two least probable symbols repeatedly; a min-heap, `heapq`; the greedy method is Stage 3, linked not re-taught) produces an optimal prefix code; for *dyadic* distributions `L = H` exactly — a worked exact example, and a non-dyadic example where `L > H` strictly** — **entropy as the fundamental limit of lossless compression (the source-coding bound; the full coding theorems `CITED [VERIFY]`)**; **the asymptotic equipartition view (closing the S4B-11 Project 01/Unit 2 link): for i.i.d. draws, `−(1/n) log p(X₁…Xₙ) → H(X)` (the law of large numbers, S4B-11 Unit 2, applied to the i.i.d. variables `−log p(Xᵢ)`) — so a long sequence's total surprisal is about `nH`, and a typical sequence has probability about `2^{−nH}`; a seeded demonstration with the running average and its standard-error band (S4B-11 Unit 4)**; **maximum entropy (proved in L3)**: **among all distributions on `K` outcomes the uniform has maximum entropy `log K`; with a constraint on the *mean score* `E[f(X)] = c`, the maximum-entropy distribution has the Gibbs/Boltzmann form `p(x) ∝ exp(λ f(x))` — **the softmax with `fᵢ = zᵢ` and a temperature** (closing S4B-10 Unit 2's cited statement); the temperature–entropy monotonicity (entropy of `softmax(z/T)` increases in `T`: `0` as `T → 0⁺` for a unique maximum and `log K` as `T → ∞` — proved in L3 by differentiation)**; **differential entropy for continuous variables**: **`h(X) = −∫ f(x) log f(x) dx` (S4B-06 integrals); **it can be negative; it is *not* invariant under a change of variables (`h(aX + b) = h(X) + log |a|`); the discretisation relation `H(X_Δ) ≈ h(X) − log Δ` for a fine bin width `Δ` (a numerical demonstration)**; **closed forms derived with the S4B-10 moments**: **`Uniform(a, b)`: `log(b − a)`; `Exponential(λ)`: `1 − ln λ` nats; `Normal(μ, σ²)`: `½ ln(2πe σ²)` nats (derived from `E[(X − μ)²] = σ²`) — each verified against S4B-06 quadrature (the S4B-06 Project 02 stretch goal, now taught) — and the maximum-entropy property of the normal among densities with a given variance (proved in L3)**; **estimating entropy from data (the plug-in estimator and its bias)**: **`Ĥ = −Σ p̂ᵢ log p̂ᵢ` from counts is *biased low* (a consequence of Jensen's inequality on the concave `−x log x`, with the exact expectation computed by enumerating all samples of a small size from a small alphabet — S4B-09 Unit 5 empirical distribution); the first-order bias `≈ −(K − 1)/(2n)` nats (the Miller–Madow correction adds `(K̂ − 1)/(2n)`; `CITED [VERIFY]`, with the exact-enumeration and seeded measurements as the evidence); mutual-information estimates are biased *high* (even for independent variables, with the exact enumeration for tiny `n`); the consequence for feature ranking by mutual information on small samples (a seeded demonstration: independent features ranked by plug-in MI, the top-ranked looks "informative" — the S4B-12 multiplicity link; permutation null calibration as the remedy)**.
- **L3:** **proofs and derivations, written in full:** (a) **`lim_{x→0⁺} x ln x = 0`: for `y > 0`, `ln y ≤ 2(√y − 1) ≤ 2√y` (from `ln t ≤ t − 1` with `t = √y`, which follows from the concavity of `ln` or from S4B-05 calculus on `t − 1 − ln t`); with `y = 1/x`, `0 ≤ x ln(1/x) ≤ 2√x → 0` — so `0 log 0 := 0` is the continuous extension**; (b) **`H ≥ 0` (each term `−p log p ≥ 0`) and `H = 0` iff all `pᵢ ∈ {0, 1}`**; (c) **`H(p) ≤ log K` with equality iff `p` is uniform: by Jensen on the concave `log` (S4B-09 Unit 7): `H(p) = Σ pᵢ log(1/pᵢ) ≤ log Σ pᵢ(1/pᵢ) = log K`, and the equality case of Jensen (strict concavity) forces `1/pᵢ` constant on the support — and the uniform on the full support**; (d) **Gibbs' inequality in its entropy form: for probability vectors `p, q` on the same finite set, `−Σ pᵢ log pᵢ ≤ −Σ pᵢ log qᵢ` with equality iff `p = q` — the S4B-10 Unit 2 proof (Jensen on `log`), restated here as the statement `H(p) ≤ H(p, q)` with the pointer that its KL name is Unit 4**; (e) **concavity of `H`: `H(λp + (1 − λ)q) ≥ λH(p) + (1 − λ)H(q)` (each term `−x log x` is concave by `d²/dx²(−x ln x) = −1/x < 0`, S4B-05)**; (f) **the binary entropy function's shape and derivative `h'(p) = log₂((1 − p)/p)` (S4B-05) — zero at `p = ½`, positive for `p < ½`**; (g) **independence ⇒ additivity of entropy (the log of a product is a sum), and the chain rule `H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X)` (`log p(x, y) = log p(x) + log p(y | x)`, take expectations)**; (h) **`I(X; Y) ≥ 0` with equality iff independence: `I(X; Y) = Σ p(x, y) log[p(x, y)/(p(x)p(y))] ≥ 0` by Gibbs' inequality applied to the joint distribution `p(x, y)` and the product distribution `p(x)p(y)` on the same support — so `H(Y | X) ≤ H(Y)` ("conditioning reduces entropy on average")**; (i) **`H(f(X)) ≤ H(X)`: the chain rule on `(X, f(X))` gives `H(X, f(X)) = H(X) + H(f(X) | X) = H(X)` (since `f(X)` is determined by `X`) and also `= H(f(X)) + H(X | f(X)) ≥ H(f(X))`**; (j) **Kraft's inequality (necessity): the codewords of a prefix code are the leaves of a binary tree; a codeword of length `l` occupies `2^{L − l}` of the `2^L` depth-`L` descendants (`L` the maximum length), and the occupied sets are disjoint, so `Σ 2^{L − lᵢ} ≤ 2^L` — full; the sufficiency (construct a code from lengths satisfying Kraft) stated with the canonical construction, `CITED [VERIFY]` or proved — say which**; (k) **the entropy lower bound for prefix codes: `L − H = Σ pᵢ log₂(pᵢ/2^{−lᵢ}) = Σ pᵢ log₂(pᵢ/qᵢ) − log₂ Σ 2^{−lᵢ}` with `qᵢ = 2^{−lᵢ}/Σ 2^{−lⱼ}`; by Gibbs (`Σ pᵢ log(pᵢ/qᵢ) ≥ 0`) and Kraft (`log₂ Σ 2^{−lⱼ} ≤ 0`), `L ≥ H`, with equality iff `pᵢ = 2^{−lᵢ}` (dyadic) — full**; (l) **the Shannon code lengths `⌈−log₂ pᵢ⌉` satisfy Kraft (since `2^{−⌈x⌉} ≤ 2^{−x}`) and give `L < H + 1`; Huffman's code is optimal among prefix codes (`CITED [VERIFY]` with the exchange-argument outline and an exhaustive verification by enumeration of all prefix-code length assignments for small alphabets: no code beats Huffman's average length)**; (m) **the asymptotic equipartition statement for i.i.d. sources: the weak law (S4B-11 Unit 2) applied to the i.i.d. variables `Yᵢ = −log p(Xᵢ)` (finite variance for a finite alphabet) gives `−(1/n) log p(X₁…Xₙ) →ᴾ H(X)` — full; ## Unit 3 — Cross-entropy as a loss

- **L1:** **cross-entropy: the average surprisal when the *true* distribution is `p` but you *use* the model `q`: `H(p, q) = −Σₓ p(x) log q(x)`** — **the average number of bits you pay if you build your code for `q` while the data come from `p`; it is at least the entropy `H(p)` (Gibbs, Unit 2) and equals it only when `q = p`**; **the *decomposition* `H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p ‖ q)`: the entropy (unavoidable uncertainty in the data) plus the extra cost of using the wrong model (Unit 4)** — **worked by hand with exact dyadic examples: `p = (½, ¼, ¼)`, `q = (¼, ¼, ½)`: `H(p) = 1.5` bits, `H(p, q) = 1.75` bits, the excess `0.25` bits**; **cross-entropy as a *loss* for classification: with a one-hot target `y` (true class `c`), `H(y, q) = −log q_c` — the negative log-probability the model gave the correct class (the log loss); the average over a dataset is the *cross-entropy loss*** — a worked example with three examples by hand and by NumPy; **binary cross-entropy: `−[y log q + (1 − y) log(1 − q)]` (closing the S4B-05 Unit 3 and S4B-10 Unit 1 previews: the negative of the Bernoulli log-likelihood)**; **the loss is `0` only for a perfectly confident correct prediction, grows without bound as the correct class's probability → `0` (a plot of `−log q_c`), and is `ln K` (`log₂ K` bits) for a uniform guess on `K` classes (the baseline every model should beat — proved in L3)**; **from logits: `CE(z, c) = log Σⱼ e^{zⱼ} − z_c = LSE(z) − z_c` (S4B-10 Unit 2: the stable form), and the gradient with respect to the logits is `softmax(z) − y` (S4B-07 Units 2 and 4, restated; closing)**; **a first look at why this loss is used: its gradient is `q − y` — large when the prediction is wrong, small when right — computed by hand for a sigmoid output: `∂CE/∂z = σ(z) − y` (S4B-05 chain rule)**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact/high-precision checks.

## Unit 4 — KL divergence

- **L1:** **the Kullback–Leibler divergence measures how much extra surprise you pay for using the model `q` when the data come from `p`: `KL(p ‖ q) = Σₓ p(x) log[p(x)/q(x)] = H(p, q) − H(p)`** — **the *excess* of the cross-entropy over the entropy (the Unit 3 decomposition, now named)**; **it is `≥ 0` and `= 0` iff `p = q` (proved in L3 — Gibbs' inequality)**; **it is *not symmetric* (`KL(p ‖ q) ≠ KL(q ‖ p)` in general) and *not a metric* (it does not satisfy the triangle inequality and is not a distance) — a worked example by hand: `p = (½, ½)` and `q = (¼, ¾)`: `KL(p ‖ q)` and `KL(q ‖ p)` computed to stated precision (high-precision `Decimal`), clearly different**; **the support condition: if `q(x) = 0` while `p(x) > 0`, then `KL(p ‖ q) = +∞` (the zero-probability rule: the model says "impossible" about something that happens); the reverse `KL(q ‖ p)` can be finite — an asymmetry with a concrete meaning**; **closed forms by hand: between two Bernoullis `KL(Bern(p) ‖ Bern(q)) = p ln(p/q) + (1 − p) ln((1 − p)/(1 − q))`; between two categoricals; between two univariate normals `KL(N(μ₁, σ₁²) ‖ N(μ₂, σ₂²)) = ln(σ₂/σ₁) + (σ₁² + (μ₁ − μ₂)²)/(2σ₂²) − ½` (nats; derived in L3)**; **KL as the cost of a mistaken code (the coding reading: `KL` bits per symbol wasted)**; **what to remember: *target first, model second*; KL is the quantity maximum-likelihood fitting minimises (Unit 3)**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact/high-precision checks.

## Unit 5 — Perplexity

- **L1:** **perplexity is the exponential of the cross-entropy: `PP = exp(H(p̂, q))` (nats) `= 2^{H(p̂, q) in bits}` — *base-independent* — where `H(p̂, q) = −(1/N)Σₜ log q(xₜ | x_{<t})` is the average per-token negative log-likelihood of a model `q` on a test sequence**; **the interpretation: *the effective number of equally likely choices the model is "hesitating between" at each step* — the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities the model assigned to the true tokens: `PP = (Πₜ 1/q(xₜ | x_{<t}))^{1/N}`**; **worked by hand**: a model that always assigns probability `¼` to the true token has `PP = 4`; a model assigning `½, ¼, ¼, ⅛` to four true tokens has `PP = (2·4·4·8)^{1/4} = 256^{1/4} = 4` (exact); a perfect model has `PP = 1`; a uniform model over a vocabulary of size `V` has `PP = V` (proved in L3); **perplexity has a floor of `1`, no ceiling, and is `+∞` if any true token has probability `0` (the zero-probability rule)**; **the perplexity of a *distribution* `p`: `2^{H(p)}` (the "effective number of outcomes" of Unit 2 — closing the S4B-11 Project 01 definition)**; **the floor for a test source: for data drawn from `p`, the best possible perplexity of *any* model is `exp(H(p))` (since `H(p, q) ≥ H(p)`, Unit 3); a model's perplexity above that floor measures `exp(KL)` — `PP(q) = exp(H(p)) · exp(KL(p ‖ q))` (proved in L3)**; **computing perplexity in code (log-space: the average of per-token negative log-probabilities, then the exponential; never a product of probabilities)**; **a first worked n-gram example on a tiny synthetic corpus (a unigram model with add-`α` smoothing: the counts, the probabilities, the per-token surprisals, the average, the perplexity — exact `Fraction` arithmetic for the probabilities and `Decimal` for the logarithm; the zero-count hazard and its smoothing fix)**; ≥ 3 worked examples with exact/high-precision checks.
