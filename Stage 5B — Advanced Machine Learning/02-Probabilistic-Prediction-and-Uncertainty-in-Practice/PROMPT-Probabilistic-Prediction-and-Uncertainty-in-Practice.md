## Conventions stated once, used everywhere

- **Claims policy.** Every claim about learning efficacy, hiring, the "best" uncertainty method, a number of replicates, or an abstention cutoff is exactly one of: (a) cited research/documentation tagged `[VERIFY]` with limits; (b) practitioner opinion/rule of thumb tagged `[JUDGEMENT]` or `HEURISTIC`; (c) a verifiable statement about mathematics, code, or the tree; or (d) omitted. **No general ranking of uncertainty methods; no claim that a method provides coverage, calibration, or conditional validity beyond what its theorem states; every empirical comparison is stated as evidence about its generator only.** Durations `[ASSUMPTION]`; sizes `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`.
- **Correctness policy.** Every worked example and key is verified **two independent ways**: (1) a hand derivation in the text, and (2) an independent computation — **exact (`Fraction`/`sympy`/enumeration/quadrature) on the tiny tables and small discrete problems wherever possible**, otherwise `numpy`/`scipy`/scikit-learn with a **stated tolerance and reason**, or simulation with stated `n`, seed, replicates, interval. **Every scoring rule, estimator, and guarantee in the module is implemented as a short spec-literal reference and cross-checked against scikit-learn/SciPy on tiny and random inputs, including ties, degenerate predictive distributions (`σ → 0`), empty calibration sets, and extreme `α`.** A library function is never the sole oracle for a claim about that function.
- **Proof policy.** Where Section 6 says "proof", write it in full: statement, hypotheses, every step justified, closing "where each hypothesis was used". **Heuristics are labelled `HEURISTIC` with their measured coverage and miss rates on a stated generator.** Where a result is `CITED [VERIFY]`, say which and give a numerical sanity check.
- **Assumed-vs-guaranteed convention (the module's central honesty device).** Every uncertainty method is stated in a table with columns **assumption → guarantee (with the theorem's hypotheses) → what is *not* guaranteed → how we measure it here**. **Marginal coverage** (averaged over `x`) and **conditional coverage** (given `x` or a group) are distinguished everywhere; **a distribution-free guarantee is marginal, finite-sample, and relies on exchangeability — and is silent about slices**.
- **Planted-truth convention.** Every uncertainty method is evaluated on generators for which **the true conditional distribution is known**, so that **true coverage (marginal and by slice), true expected score of every predictive distribution (log score, CRPS), interval/set efficiency, and the distance between the estimated and true predictive distribution are computed against truth over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds with intervals**, never inferred from the data the method saw. **The null generator and a misspecified-model generator are part of every study.**
- **Evaluation-protocol and leak-free convention (inherited from 5A-00 to 5A-13 and the 5B protocol card).** Every learned object — scalers, models, noise models, kernels and their hyperparameters, bootstrap/ensemble members, quantile regressors, **conformity-score models, and the conformal quantile** — is fitted on the rows belonging to its role: **train / calibration (`X_cal`) / test**; **the calibration set is disjoint from the training set and is used only to compute the conformal quantile (or recalibration map); the test set is sealed, touched once, and logged; coverage is always evaluated on rows that influenced neither the model nor the calibration.** **The unit of independence (row, group, time block) is stated and the splits respect it.** Every example states which items it exercises; **an example that violates one is labelled as a counterexample.**
- **Uncertainty card (the module's reporting convention; every uncertainty result is stated in this form).** **the decision and what uncertainty is for; the unit of independence and the exchangeability assumption (and its check against deployment: 5B-01 Unit 2); the data roles (train / calibration / test) and sizes; the predictive-distribution family or interval/set construction and its assumptions; the proper scores reported (log score, CRPS, Brier, pinball, interval score) with intervals; the nominal level `1 − α` and the empirical marginal coverage with an interval and the number of test rows; conditional coverage by pre-registered slices/bins with the multiplicity control; interval width or set size (efficiency); the PIT/reliability diagnostic; the abstention/review rule and its cost model where used; the seeds and replicates; the threats and the number of looks.** Reporting uncertainty without these fields is a defect; every worked example ends with the filled card.
- **Notation convention.** Data `(x_i, y_i)`; a predictive distribution `F̂(· | x)` with density/mass `p̂(· | x)`; its quantile function `Q̂_τ(x)`; a level `α` and nominal coverage `1 − α`; an interval/set `C(x)`; a calibration set of size `n` with conformity scores `s_1…s_n`; the finite-sample quantile index `⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉`; the true conditional distribution `F(· | x)` (known only in the lab); `ln` natural; sums versus means always stated; indices 1-based in text with the 0-based Python translation side by side; plain-text math with Unicode, **no LaTeX**.
- **Estimation policy.** Every reported quantity carries `n`, the data role, the seeds, and an interval: **empirical coverage by a Wilson interval on the number of test rows (with the note that coverage across calibration draws is Beta-distributed: Unit 5), proper scores by grouped bootstrap at the unit of independence, method comparisons paired by seed and generator over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds with the corrected/permutation tests of 5B-01 Unit 6, conditional coverage over pre-registered slices with Holm/BH control, and always the number of configurations and looks.** No best-seed, best-method, or best-slice reporting.

## Unit 2 — Bayesian linear and logistic regression in practice

- **L1:** **the Bayesian idea in plain words: instead of one best weight vector, keep a *distribution* over weight vectors that are plausible given the data and your prior beliefs; predictions average over that distribution, so they carry uncertainty about the weights (epistemic) as well as noise**; **Bayesian linear regression: a Gaussian prior on the weights plus Gaussian noise gives a Gaussian posterior and a Gaussian predictive distribution in closed form; with little data the posterior is wide, with lots of data it tightens**; **the connection to ridge regression: the posterior *mean* equals the ridge solution with `λ = σ²/τ²` (noise variance over prior variance)**; **Bayesian logistic regression: no closed form; the *Laplace approximation* replaces the posterior by a Gaussian centred at the penalised-likelihood solution (MAP) with the curvature (Hessian) as its precision; predictions are then "softened" toward 0.5 when the weights are uncertain**; **credible intervals vs confidence intervals in one paragraph (a probability statement about parameters given the prior and data vs a procedure with long-run coverage)**; **a first worked example by hand: a one-feature regression with three points and a stated prior: the posterior mean and variance of the slope; the predictive mean and variance at a new `x` (computed with `Fraction`); the same with `BayesianRidge`-style code; then a two-parameter logistic example with its Laplace approximation and the softened prediction**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **Bayesian regression in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the conjugate model** (prior `w ~ N(0, τ²I)`, noise `ε ~ N(0, σ²)`; posterior covariance `S = (XᵀX/σ² + I/τ²)^{-1}` and mean `m = SXᵀy/σ²`; the MAP by Newton/IRLS with a Gaussian prior (5A-05 logistic regression recalled); the **Laplace approximation** `N(w_MAP, H^{-1})` with `H = XᵀRX + I/τ²`, `R = diag(p_i(1 − p_i))`; the **predictive distribution**: the linear score `a = xᵀw` is approximately `N(xᵀw_MAP, xᵀH^{-1}x)`, and the **probit approximation** `E[σ(a)] ≈ σ(μ_a/√(1 + πσ²_a/8))`; a Monte-Carlo check of the approximation; when it is poor (skewed or multi-modal posteriors; separable data with weak priors)); **(5) a tiny sampler** (random-walk Metropolis for a two- or three-parameter logistic model, written from scratch: proposal scale tuning by acceptance rate, burn-in, thinning, effective sample size, and convergence checks (`R̂`-style across chains named); ## Unit 3 — Ensembles and the bootstrap for prediction uncertainty

- **L1:** **the bootstrap in plain words: resample your training rows with replacement many times, refit the model on each resample, and look at how much the predictions vary; the variation shows how sensitive the prediction is to the *particular sample* you got (epistemic uncertainty), but **not** how noisy the outcome itself is (aleatoric)**; **ensembles (bagged trees, random forests, gradient-boosted ensembles) give many predictions per input; their spread is an uncertainty signal with known blind spots**; **confidence interval vs prediction interval: a confidence interval for the mean prediction at `x` is narrower than a prediction interval for a new outcome at `x`, because the latter must also include the outcome's own noise**; **out-of-bag (OOB) predictions: each bootstrap sample leaves out about 37% of the rows, which gives honest held-out predictions for free**; **a first worked example by hand: a six-point dataset and five bootstrap resamples (listed): the five fitted slopes, their spread, the OOB residuals, and a prediction interval that adds the residual spread to the bootstrap spread; compared with the Bayesian predictive of Unit 2**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **bootstrap and ensemble uncertainty in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) bootstrap flavours** (pairs/case resampling; residual resampling; wild bootstrap for heteroscedasticity (named); parametric bootstrap; ## Unit 5 — Split conformal prediction

- **L1:** **conformal prediction turns *any* predictor into one that comes with a prediction interval (or set) that is guaranteed to contain the truth about `1 − α` of the time — without assuming a distribution for the errors — as long as the new data are *exchangeable* with the data used to calibrate (loosely: drawn the same way)**; **the recipe, in plain words: (1) split the data into a *training* part and a *calibration* part; (2) fit any model on the training part; (3) on the calibration part compute a *conformity score* for each row (for regression: the absolute error `|y − μ̂(x)|`); (4) take a specific high quantile of those scores (the `⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉`-th smallest of `n` scores); (5) for a new `x`, the interval is `μ̂(x) ± that quantile`**; **for classification: a *prediction set* of classes whose probabilities are high enough that the true class is in the set about `1 − α` of the time**; **what the guarantee is and is not: it holds *on average* over new test points (marginal coverage), not for each individual or each subgroup; and it is a statement about the procedure, not about this particular calibration draw**; **a first worked example by hand: `n = 9` calibration scores; `α = 0.2`; the index `⌈(9 + 1)(0.8)⌉ = 8`; the 8th smallest score; the interval at a new point; then why `⌈n(1 − α)⌉ = 8` coincidentally agrees here and differs at other `n`**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **split conformal prediction in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the procedure and its data roles** (train / calibration (`X_cal`) / test; sizes; the calibration set must not be used for fitting *anything* that feeds the score except in the stated way; - **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the rank argument: for exchangeable scores `s_1,…,s_n, s_{n+1}` with no ties, the rank of `s_{n+1}` among all `n + 1` is uniform on `{1,…,n + 1}`; hence `P(s_{n+1} ≤ s_{(k)}) = k/(n + 1)` for the `k`-th order statistic of the first `n`** — complete (exchangeability ⇒ every permutation equally likely); ## Unit 6 — Conformalized quantile regression and adaptive intervals

- **L1:** **split conformal with absolute residuals gives intervals of the same width everywhere; real data are noisier in some places than others; we want wide intervals where the outcome is hard to predict and narrow ones where it is easy — *and* we still want the coverage guarantee**; **conformalized quantile regression (CQR) in plain words: (1) train two quantile models on the training set, one for a low quantile (say 5%) and one for a high quantile (95%); (2) on the calibration set measure by how much the true value falls *outside* the quantile band (negative if inside); (3) take the right quantile of those amounts; (4) widen (or narrow) the band by that amount**; **what you get: intervals whose width varies with the input *and* a marginal coverage guarantee; what you do not get: coverage *for every* input**; **group-conditional ("Mondrian") conformal in plain words: run the recipe separately inside each group so the guarantee holds *within each group* (if each group has enough calibration points)**; **a first worked example by hand: five calibration points with quantile bands; the conformity scores `E_i = max(q_lo − y, y − q_hi)`; the correction `Q̂` at `α = 0.2`; the adjusted interval at a new point; then Mondrian by two groups with their own corrections**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
