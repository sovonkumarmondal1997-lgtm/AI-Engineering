- **Causal-claim convention (stated once; applied everywhere).** **Every causal analysis states, in this order: (i) the decision and the *estimand* (ATE, ATT, ATC, CATE `τ(x)`, LATE, policy value) for a named population, treatment, comparison, outcome, and horizon; (ii) the potential-outcome or graphical model; (iii) the *identification assumptions* (SUTVA/no interference and consistency; ignorability/unconfoundedness given a stated adjustment set; positivity/overlap; parallel trends; exclusion and monotonicity; continuity; stable allocation), each labelled *testable (with the test and its limits)* or *untestable (with a sensitivity analysis)*; (iv) the design (randomised or observational) and the data roles; (v) the estimator, its variance estimator, and the unit of independence (clusters named); (vi) diagnostics (balance, overlap, pre-trends, first stage, density/continuity, sample-ratio check); (vii) sensitivity to unmeasured confounding or assumption violations; (viii) the planted-truth check where available; and (ix) the decision rule with its costs.** An association, a regression coefficient, a feature importance, a SHAP value, or a counterfactual explanation is never reported as a causal effect. Words such as "impact", "driver", "lift", and "because" are reserved for estimates accompanied by items (i)–(iii).
- **Planted-truth convention.** Every estimator, diagnostic, and test is evaluated on generators for which **the truth is known**: both potential outcomes for every unit (hence the true ATE/ATT/CATE and the true value of any policy); the true propensity function; the true graph; the true violation magnitudes (parallel-trend gap, exclusion violation, manipulation, interference); the true complier share and LATE; the true jump at the cutoff; the true arm means and optimal arm; so that **bias, variance, RMSE, interval coverage, type I error, power, balance, overlap, policy value, and regret are measured against truth over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds with intervals**, never inferred from the data the method saw. **Both nulls are part of every study: the zero-effect null (false-positive rate and coverage of zero) and the pure-confounding null (association without effect: any "effect" a method finds is bias).**

## Unit 1 — Prediction versus causal inference

- **L1:** **prediction asks "what outcome will this customer have?"; causal inference asks "what would happen to this customer's outcome *if we changed something*?" A model can predict very well and still say nothing about what an action would do. The *potential-outcomes* idea: every unit has two outcomes, `Y(1)` (if treated) and `Y(0)` (if not); we can only ever observe one of them, so the individual effect `τ = Y(1) − Y(0)` is never observed — the *fundamental problem of causal inference***; **the main targets (*estimands*): ATE (average over everyone), ATT (average over those actually treated), CATE `τ(x)` (average for people like `x`)**; **a first worked example by hand: an eight-unit table with *both* potential outcomes (low-type units have `Y(0) = 2`, effect `+1`; high-type units have `Y(0) = 6`, effect `+3`); five units are treated (one low-type, four high-type): the naive difference in means is `7.8 − 2 = 5.8`, the true ATE is `2`, the ATT `2.6`, and the gap is the *selection bias* `3.2` (computed exactly)**; **Simpson's paradox: a treatment can look worse overall and better within every subgroup (a four-cell table: treated `68/120` versus control `93/120` overall, yet `0.90` versus `0.85` in the mild group and `0.50` versus `0.40` in the severe group)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **prediction versus causation in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the questions** (association `P(Y | T)`, intervention `P(Y | do(T))`, counterfactual `Y_i(1)` for a unit that got `0`; the *ladder* of association, intervention, counterfactual: named `CITED [VERIFY]` Pearl); **(2) the potential-outcomes framework** (units, treatments, potential outcomes; **SUTVA** = no interference (one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome) + one version of each treatment; **consistency** `Y = Y(T)`; estimands ATE, ATT, ATC, CATE, and the *population* and *horizon* each refers to; the unit-level effect is not identifiable; averages can be); **(3) why a good predictor is not a causal answer** (a model trained on `(X, T) → Y` learns `E[Y | X, T]`, which equals `E[Y(T) | X]` only under ignorability; predictions at `T = 1` versus `T = 0` for the same `X` can differ because of confounding, proxies, and selection; **feature importance, SHAP values, partial dependence, and counterfactual explanations are statements about the fitted model** (5B-07 Units 5–10) — the module's planted generators show importance without effect and effect without importance); **(4) the naive comparison decomposed** (`E[Y | T = 1] − E[Y | T = 0] = ATT + selection bias`, with `selection bias = E[Y(0) | T = 1] − E[Y(0) | T = 0]` (L3); the sign and size of selection bias from targeting rules: a coupon sent to likely buyers inflates the naive effect); **(5) Simpson's paradox and aggregation** (the reversal as an arithmetic fact about weights; the causal question determines whether to stratify or not (L3 and Unit 2: it depends on the causal graph); a stratified analysis is not always right: conditioning on a collider recreates the paradox); **(6) randomisation as the gold standard and what it buys** (random assignment makes `T ⊥ (Y(0), Y(1))`, so the naive difference is unbiased (L3); what it does not buy: external validity, compliance, no interference, no attrition bias, correct inference when the unit of randomisation differs from the unit of analysis); **(7) observational data and the role of ML** (ML helps *estimate nuisance functions* (propensity, outcome) and *flexibly adjust*; it does not remove the need for identification assumptions; predictive accuracy is not a validation of a causal estimate); **(8) validating causal estimates** (the counterfactual is never observed, so *held-out outcome accuracy cannot validate an effect estimate*; validation uses planted truth, randomised benchmarks (A/B data as ground truth for an observational method: the "benchmarking" design, named `CITED [VERIFY]`), placebo/negative-control outcomes, and diagnostics); **(9) the decision-first view** (the estimand is chosen from the decision: ship or not (ATE), who to target (CATE), whether it worked after the fact (ATT); a mismatch between estimand and decision is a design error); **(10) reporting** (the causal card with the estimand and assumptions first)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the naive-difference decomposition: `E[Y | T = 1] − E[Y | T = 0] = E[Y(1) | T = 1] − E[Y(0) | T = 0] = (E[Y(1) | T = 1] − E[Y(0) | T = 1]) + (E[Y(0) | T = 1] − E[Y(0) | T = 0]) = ATT + selection bias` (add and subtract `E[Y(0) | T = 1]`; consistency used for the first equality)** — complete; (b) **`ATE = P(T = 1)·ATT + P(T = 0)·ATC` (the law of total expectation over `T`)** — complete; (c) **randomisation makes the naive difference unbiased: if `T ⊥ (Y(0), Y(1))` then `E[Y(0) | T = 1] = E[Y(0) | T = 0]`, the selection bias is `0`, and `E[Y | T = 1] − E[Y | T = 0] = ATT = ATE` (the last equality because `E[Y(1) − Y(0) | T = 1] = E[Y(1) − Y(0)]` under independence)** — complete; (d) **the worked table: `Y(0) = (2, 2, 2, 2, 6, 6, 6, 6)`, `τ = (1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 3, 3, 3)`, treated = units `{1, 5, 6, 7, 8}`: observed treated mean `(3 + 4·9)/5 = 7.8`, control mean `2`, naive `5.8`; `ATE = 2`; `ATT = (1 + 4·3)/5 = 2.6`; selection bias `(2 + 4·6)/5 − 2 = 3.2`; check `2.6 + 3.2 = 5.8`** — complete (computed exactly); (e) **Simpson's paradox arithmetic: treated `18/20` (mild) and `50/100` (severe), control `85/100` and `8/20`: overall `68/120 = 0.567` versus `93/120 = 0.775`; within strata `0.90 > 0.85` and `0.50 > 0.40`; standardising to the pooled strata sizes (`120` mild, `120` severe) gives `0.70` versus `0.625`, an adjusted difference of `+0.075`; the reversal arises because the treated group is mostly severe cases (`100/120`) and the control group mostly mild (`100/120`)** — complete (computed); (f) **the individual effect is not identified: for any distribution of observed `(T, Y)`, a joint distribution of `(Y(0), Y(1))` consistent with the marginals can be built with the effect `τ` anywhere in a Fréchet-type band; a two-unit example shows two tables with identical observed data and different `τ_i`** — complete (construct the two tables); **counterexamples**: **a naive difference of `5.8` for a true ATE of `2` (computed)**; **Simpson's reversal (computed)**; **a predictive model with high accuracy and a wrong sign for the treatment contribution under confounding (seeded)**; **two tables with identical observed data and different unit effects (computed)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal potential-outcome simulators, naive/ATE/ATT/selection-bias decompositions, and standardisation vs NumPy and exact enumeration (agreement conditions: weights, tie handling)**; **tool card** for `causallab.po` (potential-outcome generators with both outcomes, true ATE/ATT/ATC/CATE, and known assignment mechanisms; naive/ATT/selection-bias decomposers; Simpson/standardisation tools; randomised and confounded assignment modes; manifests; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).
- **L4:** **causal thinking in the ML workflow (an MLOps lens)**: **separating *prediction products* (scores) from *decision products* (actions) in the model registry: a model card states whether any causal use is permitted and under which assumptions; logging assignment and exposure as first-class data (propensities, randomisation keys, timestamps: the data contract for later causal analysis; link 5D `03`); experiment-versus-observation governance (what may be decided from observational analyses, with a documented review); treating "impact" language as claims requiring a causal card; avoiding silent feedback of causal claims into training data (Unit 9)**; hostile inputs (treatment indicators defined after the outcome; outcomes measured at different horizons by arm; units appearing in both arms; non-binary or dose-like treatments coded as binary; missing treatment status; interference between units); **exhaustive verification** of the decomposition and the total-expectation identity **over all small potential-outcome tables and assignments (exact `Fraction`)**, of the Simpson arithmetic **over all small 2×2×2 tables (counting reversals)**, and of unbiasedness under randomisation **by enumerating all assignments of tiny tables** (domains named and sized; `EVIDENCE ONLY`); mutation lab (naive difference reported as the ATE; ATT and ATE swapped; post-treatment covariate used in standardisation; assignment correlated with a potential outcome in the "randomised" generator; stratification applied to a collider; held-out outcome accuracy used to validate an effect estimate) with no surviving mutants; case study: a retention model's "driver" list used to justify an intervention that did nothing, and a before/after "lift" that was the selection of who received the offer (original synthetic reproductions with a causal card and a randomised holdout as fixes).

## Unit 2 — Confounding and causal diagrams

- **L1:** **a *confounder* is a variable that influences both the treatment and the outcome, so that treated and untreated groups differ even without any treatment effect (sicker patients get the drug *and* do worse). A *causal diagram* (DAG) draws an arrow from each cause to its effect; it tells us which variables to adjust for — and which *not* to**; **three building blocks: a *fork* `T ← U → Y` (a common cause: adjust for `U`), a *chain* `T → M → Y` (a mediator: adjusting for `M` removes the part of the effect that flows through it), a *collider* `T → C ← Y` (a common effect: adjusting for `C` creates a false association)**; **a first worked example by hand: a linear world `U → T`, `U → Y`, `T → Y` with `T = U + ε`, `Y = 2T + U + η`, unit variances: the regression slope of `Y` on `T` is `2.5` (not the true `2`), and `2.0` after adjusting for `U` (computed exactly); then a mediator world where total effect `= 2` but adjusting for the mediator gives the direct effect `1`**; **the rule: choose adjustment variables from the *causal story*, not from "everything available"**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **confounding and graphs in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) causal DAGs** (nodes, arrows as direct causal influences, absence of an arrow as a strong assumption; structural equations `V = f_V(parents, noise)`; the DAG encodes the *conditional independences* it implies and the *interventional* structure; the module states every DAG as an assumption, never as a discovery); **(2) paths and d-separation** (a path is *blocked* by conditioning on a non-collider on it, or by *not* conditioning on a collider (or its descendants); `X ⊥ Y | Z` is implied iff `Z` d-separates them (`CITED [VERIFY]`); the learner implements d-separation by a path-enumeration algorithm for small DAGs and checks it against numeric conditional independence in linear-Gaussian simulations); **(3) the back-door criterion and adjustment sets** (a set `Z` satisfies the back-door criterion relative to `(T, Y)` if it blocks every back-door path and contains no descendant of `T`; then `P(y | do(t)) = Σ_zP(y | t, z)P(z)` (L3); **minimal and alternative adjustment sets**; the learner's enumerator of valid adjustment sets for small DAGs); **(4) what not to adjust for** (**mediators** (blocks part of the effect: total versus direct effect; L3), **colliders** (opens a path: collider bias recalled from 5B-07 Unit 9 (c): `Cov(X, Z | C) = −Var(X)Var(Z)/Var(C)`), **M-bias** (a pre-treatment collider: adjusting for it *creates* bias: a computed linear example), **instruments and "bad controls"** (adjusting for a variable that affects only `T` inflates variance and amplifies bias from unmeasured confounding: named, measured), **post-treatment variables in general**); **(5) unmeasured confounding** (when no valid adjustment set is observed; the *omitted-variable bias formula* recalled from 5B-07 Unit 9: slope `= β + γ·Cov(T, U)/Var(T)`; direction and size from assumed effects; **sensitivity analysis** (Unit 3) instead of silence); **(6) other identification strategies on graphs** (front-door adjustment (named; one worked example), instrumental variables (Unit 7), do-calculus rules (named `CITED [VERIFY]`)); **(7) selection and conditioning on the sample** (selection bias as conditioning on a collider: a sample that only includes users who *engaged* conditions on a common effect of treatment and outcome; survivorship; attrition; a computed example); **(8) confounding in ML pipelines** (features in a model that are descendants of the treatment; leakage as post-treatment adjustment; proxies; *the same variable can be a confounder in one question and a mediator in another*: the question fixes the role); **(9) drawing and defending a DAG** (domain knowledge; timing as a guide (causes precede effects); the "what would falsify it" list: testable implications (conditional independences) versus untestable ones; DAG sensitivity: alternative DAGs reported); **(10) reporting** (the DAG, the adjustment set with reasons for each inclusion/exclusion, the testable implications checked, the unmeasured-confounding discussion, and the sensitivity analysis)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **back-door adjustment: if `Y(t) ⊥ T | Z` (conditional ignorability, which the back-door criterion implies in a DAG-faithful SCM) and `P(T = t | Z = z) > 0` whenever `P(Z = z) > 0` (positivity), then `P(Y(t) = y) = Σ_zP(Y(t) = y | Z = z)P(z) = Σ_zP(Y(t) = y | T = t, Z = z)P(z) = Σ_zP(Y = y | T = t, Z = z)P(z)` (law of total probability; ignorability; consistency)** — complete; (b) **the confounded linear world: `U ∼ N(0, 1)`, `T = U + ε` (`Var ε = 1`), `Y = 2T + U + η`: `Cov(T, Y) = 2Var(T) + Cov(T, U) = 2·2 + 1 = 5`, `Var(T) = 2`, so the regression slope is `5/2 = 2.5 = 2 + 1·Cov(T, U)/Var(T)`; adjusting for `U` gives slope `2`** — complete (computed exactly; matches the omitted-variable formula); (c) **mediator: `T → M → Y` and `T → Y` with `M = aT + ε_M`, `Y = bM + cT + η`, total effect `c + ab`, direct effect `c`; for `a = 0.5`, `b = 2`, `c = 1`: total `2`, adjusting for `M` gives `1`** — complete (substitute); (d) **collider (recalled and used): `X ⊥ Z`, `C = X + Z + ε`; `Cov(X, Z | C) = −Var(X)Var(Z)/Var(C)` (Gaussian conditioning: `Cov(X, Z | C) = Cov(X, Z) − Cov(X, C)Cov(C, Z)/Var(C)`); ## Unit 3 — Treatment effects and selection bias

## Unit 4 — Propensity scores, matching, and weighting

- **L1:** **when treatment was not randomised but we have measured everything that drove who got treated (`X`), we can *adjust*: compare treated and untreated units *with the same `X`*. The *propensity score* `e(x) = P(T = 1 | X = x)` squeezes all of `X` into one number: units with the same propensity are comparable. Two ways to use it: *matching* (pair each treated unit with an untreated unit of similar propensity) and *weighting* (give each unit weight `1/e` if treated, `1/(1 − e)` if not, to make the groups look alike)**; **a first worked example by hand: a binary covariate `x` (half of the units each); `P(T = 1 | x = 0) = 0.2`, `P(T = 1 | x = 1) = 0.8`; mean outcomes `Y(0) = 1` and `Y(1) = 2` when `x = 0`, `Y(0) = 5` and `Y(1) = 6` when `x = 1` (true ATE `1`): the naive difference is `5.2 − 1.8 = 3.4`; inverse-propensity weights `5` and `1.25` give back `1` exactly (computed)**; **three checks are mandatory: *overlap* (every kind of unit has a chance of both arms), *balance* (after weighting or matching the groups look alike on `X`), and *weight sanity* (no handful of units carrying the estimate)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **propensity methods in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the assumptions** (**unconfoundedness given `X`**: all variables that influence both `T` and `Y` are measured and pre-treatment; **positivity**; ## Unit 5 — Difference-in-differences and synthetic control

- **L1:** **sometimes a change hits one group at a known time — a pricing policy in some regions, a feature launched in some markets — and we observe both groups *before* and *after*. A simple before/after comparison confuses the effect with everything else that changed over time; a treated-versus-control comparison after the change confuses it with pre-existing differences. *Difference-in-differences* (DiD) takes the *change over time in the treated group* minus the *change over time in the control group*: the control group's change stands in for what would have happened to the treated group anyway**; **a first worked example by hand: treated group average `10` before and `16` after; control `8` before and `11` after; DiD `= (16 − 10) − (11 − 8) = 3`; the before/after change alone would say `6`, the after-only gap `5`; if the treated group would have grown by `5` without the policy (a trend the control group does not share) the true effect is `1`, not `3` — the *parallel trends* assumption is what DiD rests on (computed exactly)**; **a *synthetic control* builds a custom comparison from a weighted mix of several untreated units that tracked the treated unit well before the change**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **DiD and synthetic control in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the setting and estimand** (units observed over time; treatment starts at `t₀` for some units; the estimand is the **ATT** at post-treatment times; pre-period trends give *evidence*, not proof**); **(2) the 2×2 DiD** (the estimator and its regression form `Y_{it} = α + β·Treated_i + γ·Post_t + δ·(Treated_i × Post_t) + ε_{it}`, where `δ` is the DiD estimate (L3); **(3) event studies** (leads and lags of treatment time: coefficients `β_k` for `k = −K,…,L` relative to treatment; **pre-treatment coefficients near zero support parallel trends but have low power and can mislead (a measured power study)**; normalising to the period before treatment; **no pre-testing as the only defence**: a documented sensitivity analysis to a stated trend violation (a linear differential trend: L3)); **(4) violations and robustness** (differential trends; Ashenfelter-dip-style selection into treatment based on a recent shock (named); composition changes; placebo treatments in untreated periods or units (permutation of treatment dates/units: the learner's placebo distribution); ## Unit 6 — Uplift modelling and heterogeneous treatment effects

- **L1:** **an *uplift* (or heterogeneous-treatment-effect, HTE) model estimates how much an action *changes* the outcome for each kind of unit — the CATE `τ(x)` — so we can treat those who benefit most (or avoid those who are harmed). ## Unit 7 — Instrumental variables and regression discontinuity (introduction)

- **L1:** **when we cannot measure every confounder, two quasi-experimental ideas can still give causal answers under their own assumptions. An *instrument* `Z` nudges treatment but affects the outcome *only through* treatment (a randomised encouragement, a lottery, a server outage). A *regression discontinuity* (RD) uses a *cutoff rule*: units just above and just below a threshold on a score are almost identical, but one side gets the treatment**; **a first worked example by hand: a randomised encouragement `Z`; `60%` of encouraged and `20%` of non-encouraged units use a feature; average outcomes `5.8` (encouraged) and `5.0` (not): the effect of *being encouraged* is `0.8`; because the encouragement changed usage by `0.4`, the effect of *using* the feature for the units that responded to the nudge is `0.8/0.4 = 2` (the *Wald* estimate; computed exactly). RD: units with a score below the cutoff `c` do not get the benefit and units above it do; the *jump* in the average outcome at `c` is the effect for units at the cutoff**; **what these give is a *local* answer (for compliers, or near the cutoff), not an average for everyone**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **IV and RD in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) instrumental variables** (assumptions: **relevance** (`Z` moves `T`), **independence/as-if randomness** (`Z ⊥ (Y(·), T(·))`), **exclusion** (`Z` affects `Y` only through `T`), **monotonicity** (no defiers); **the LATE theorem**: the Wald ratio identifies the average effect for *compliers* (L3); the four principal strata (always-takers, never-takers, compliers, defiers); *which* units are compliers is not observed; **LATE ≠ ATE** and depends on the instrument); **(2) estimation** (**Wald** ratio `(E[Y | Z = 1] − E[Y | Z = 0])/(E[T | Z = 1] − E[T | Z = 0])`; **two-stage least squares (2SLS)**: regress `T` on `Z` and covariates, regress `Y` on the fitted `T̂` (the learner's implementation; **standard errors must account for the first-stage estimation: naive second-stage OLS standard errors are wrong**; heteroskedasticity-robust variance); the **reduced form** and **first stage** reported separately; with one binary instrument and no covariates 2SLS equals Wald (L3)); **(3) weak instruments** (a weak first stage (small `E[T | Z = 1] − E[T | Z = 0]`) makes 2SLS biased toward OLS and its intervals unreliable; the **first-stage F-statistic** as a diagnostic (the "rule of 10" is a convention, `HEURISTIC`: measured behaviour on the generators); weak-instrument-robust intervals named (Anderson–Rubin: `CITED [VERIFY]`); **a measured study of 2SLS bias and coverage as the first-stage strength varies**); **(4) exclusion violations and falsification** (exclusion is untestable; **a violation of size `γ` biases the Wald estimate by `γ/π_c`** (L3); *overidentification tests* with several instruments (named), *placebo outcomes*, and the *plausibility argument* from the mechanism; **sensitivity analysis**: how large `γ` would explain the result); **(5) where instruments come from** (randomised encouragement and *intention-to-treat as an instrument for treatment received*; lotteries; outages and rollouts; geography and timing (typically weaker and contested); **"is it as-if random, and does it affect the outcome in other ways?" must be argued, not assumed**); **(6) regression discontinuity (sharp)** (a running variable `R`, cutoff `c`, treatment `T = 1{R ≥ c}`; **identification under continuity of the potential-outcome means at `c`** (L3); the estimand is the ATE *at the cutoff*; **local linear regression** within bandwidth `h` with separate slopes on each side (the learner's implementation with a triangular or uniform kernel); **bandwidth as a bias–variance trade-off**: local constants are biased by slope × `h` (L3), local linear removes first-order bias, smaller `h` raises variance; data-driven bandwidth selection named `CITED [VERIFY]`; **inference** with robust bias-corrected intervals named; **covariate balance at the cutoff, density continuity (manipulation tests: McCrary-type: named), and placebo cutoffs** as diagnostics; discrete running variables and clustering (named)); **(7) fuzzy RD** (the cutoff changes the *probability* of treatment: the ratio of the outcome jump to the treatment-probability jump is an IV estimate (LATE at the cutoff); the same weak-instrument concerns); **(8) other designs, briefly** (kinks (named), interrupted time series (Unit 5 and `../08-…`), Mendelian-randomisation-style ideas (named; out of scope)); **(9) external validity** (LATE and cutoff effects do not extrapolate; what decision they can inform (a marginal change of the cutoff or of the encouragement; not the effect of universal treatment)); **(10) reporting** (design, estimand (LATE/RD-at-cutoff), assumption arguments and tests, first stage and reduced form, estimator and variance, bandwidth and sensitivity, diagnostics, planted-truth check)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the LATE theorem (binary `Z`, `T`; assumptions: independence, exclusion, monotonicity `T(1) ≥ T(0)`, relevance `P(T(1) > T(0)) > 0`): `E[Y | Z = 1] − E[Y | Z = 0] = E[Y(T(1)) − Y(T(0))] = E[(T(1) − T(0))(Y(1) − Y(0))] = P(complier)·E[Y(1) − Y(0) | complier]`, because `T(1) − T(0) ∈ {0, 1}` by monotonicity and equals `1` exactly for compliers; **counterexamples**: **a Wald estimate off by `0.5` from a small exclusion violation (computed)**; **2SLS with a weak first stage centred near the OLS bias with wide, mis-covering intervals (seeded)**; ## Unit 8 — Variance reduction (CUPED) and sequential testing

- **L1:** **an A/B test compares two randomly assigned groups. Two engineering problems dominate: *noise* (a small effect hides inside large user-to-user variation, so tests need huge samples) and *impatience* (people peek at the results every day and stop when the number looks good, which makes false wins common). *CUPED* uses a *pre-experiment* measurement of the same users (last month's spend) to subtract predictable variation, so the same effect can be detected with fewer users. *Sequential testing* gives rules for looking repeatedly without inflating false positives**; **a first worked example by hand: outcome standard deviation `10`, a target effect of `1`, `α = 0.05`, power `80%`: `n = 2(1.96 + 0.84)²·10²/1² = 1,568` per arm; if the pre-experiment covariate correlates `0.7` with the outcome, CUPED cuts the variance by `1 − 0.49 = 0.51` and the sample to about `800` per arm (computed exactly); peeking: two equally spaced looks at `α = 0.05` each already give a false-positive rate of `8.3%`, five looks `14%`**; **a first check on any A/B test: the *sample ratio* (are the two groups the size we planned?) — a mismatch means something broke before we even look at the outcome**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **variance reduction and sequential testing in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the A/B test as a randomised experiment** (Unit 3 estimators; the sample-size formula `n = 2(z_{α/2} + z_β)²σ²/δ²` per arm for a continuous outcome and its proportion version (recalled from 5B-09 Unit 10); - **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **sample size: for a two-sided test of `H₀: δ = 0` with equal arms, outcome variance `σ²`, `Var(δ̂) = 2σ²/n`; rejecting when `|δ̂|/√(2σ²/n) > z_{α/2}` and requiring power `1 − β` at the alternative `δ` gives `δ/√(2σ²/n) = z_{α/2} + z_β` (ignoring the opposite tail), so `n = 2(z_{α/2} + z_β)²σ²/δ²`; `σ = 10`, `δ = 1`, `z_{α/2} + z_β = 2.8`: `2 × 7.84 × 100 = 1,568` per arm** — complete (normal approximation stated); (b) **CUPED unbiasedness and variance: for `Ỹ_i = Y_i − θ(X_i − μ_X)` with `X` measured before assignment, `E[X | T = 1] = E[X | T = 0] = μ_X`, so `E[Ỹ | T = 1] − E[Ỹ | T = 0] = E[Y | T = 1] − E[Y | T = 0]` (the ATE); `Var(Ỹ) = Var(Y) − 2θCov(Y, X) + θ²Var(X)`, minimised at `θ* = Cov(Y, X)/Var(X)` (set the derivative to zero), giving `Var(Ỹ) = Var(Y)(1 − ρ²)` (using `Cov(Y, X)² = ρ²Var(Y)Var(X)`); `ρ = 0.7`: `0.51`; the required sample size scales by the same factor: `1,568 × 0.51 ≈ 800`; (using the sample mean `X̄` in place of `μ_X` changes the estimator by an `O(1/n)` term that is treated as negligible and checked by simulation)** — complete; (c) **post-treatment covariates break it: if `X` is affected by treatment, `E[X | T = 1] ≠ E[X | T = 0]` and the adjusted difference equals `ATE − θ(E[X | T = 1] − E[X | T = 0])`, biased by `θ` times the covariate's own treatment effect** — complete; (d) **peeking at `K = 2` looks (information fractions `½` and `1`): `Z₁` and `Z₂` are standard normal with correlation `√(1/2)` under the null; the probability that neither `|Z₁|` nor `|Z₂|` exceeds `1.96` is `0.917` (bivariate-normal integration), so the type I error is `0.083` (computed by quadrature)** — complete (computed numerically; the correlation derivation `Cov(S_{n/2}, S_n) = n/2`); (e) **Bonferroni across `K` looks: rejecting if any look has p-value `≤ α/K` has `P(reject | H₀) ≤ Σ_kP(p_k ≤ α/K) = α` (union bound); with `K = 5`: per-look critical value `z_{0.995} = 2.576`; conservative because of the positive correlation between looks** — complete; (f) **mSPRT/Ville: under `H₀` the likelihood ratio `Λ_n = Π f₁(x_i)/f₀(x_i)` (and any mixture of such over `f₁`) is a non-negative martingale with `E[Λ_n] = 1`; (g) **SRM arithmetic: planned `50/50`, observed `50,000 + 50,600`: expected `50,300` per arm, `χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E = 2 × 300²/50,300 = 3.58`, one degree of freedom, `p = 0.0585`; the same relative imbalance with `10×` the traffic gives `χ² = 35.8` and `p ≈ 2×10⁻⁹`** — complete (computed); (h) **the winner's curse in sequential stopping: under the null with a stopping rule that stops at the first `|Z_k| > c`, the stopped estimate satisfies `|Z_τ| > c`, so the reported effect is biased away from zero by construction; for `c = 2.4`, the conditional mean of `|Z_τ|` exceeds `2.4`** — complete (selection argument; numerical size by simulation); **counterexamples**: **a 5-look peeking procedure with a `14%` false-positive rate (simulation)**; ## Unit 9 — Bandits and off-policy evaluation

- **L1:** **an A/B test splits traffic evenly for the whole test, even when one variant is clearly worse. A *multi-armed bandit* adapts: it shows the variants that look better more often while still *exploring* the others, so fewer users get the worse option. The cost of not always choosing the best arm is called *regret***; **algorithms in plain words: *ε-greedy* (with probability `ε` pick a random arm, otherwise the best so far), *UCB* (pick the arm with the highest *optimistic* estimate: mean plus an uncertainty bonus), *Thompson sampling* (draw a plausible value for each arm from its posterior and pick the largest)**; **a first worked example by hand: two arms with success rates `0.5` and `0.6` and `1,000` rounds: always splitting 50/50 loses `50` successes relative to always choosing the best arm; a Beta–Bernoulli posterior `Beta(2, 1)` (one success, no failure) versus an arm with no data (`Beta(1, 1)`): Thompson sampling picks the first arm with probability `2/3` (computed exactly)**; **a caution: because the allocation depends on past results, the *data* are no longer an unbiased sample — a tiny three-round example shows both arm means estimated too low — and the *logged propensities* are needed for any later evaluation**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **bandits and off-policy evaluation in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the stochastic bandit problem** (arms `a = 1,…,K`, rewards with means `μ_a`; best arm `a*`; gaps `Δ_a = μ_{a*} − μ_a`; **cumulative regret** `R_T = Σ_t(μ_{a*} − μ_{a_t})`; the exploration–exploitation trade-off; **A/B testing as uniform exploration** (regret linear in `T`); the objective differs from inference: bandits minimise regret, A/B tests estimate effects: a measured study of *regret versus inference quality* on the generators); **(2) algorithms** (**ε-greedy** (fixed `ε` has linear regret: L3; decaying `ε_t`); **UCB1** (index `x̄_a + √(2 ln t/n_a)`; the Hoeffding derivation: L3; logarithmic regret `CITED [VERIFY]`); **Thompson sampling** (Beta–Bernoulli conjugacy: L3; Gaussian variants named); **contextual bandits** (LinUCB and linear Thompson sampling named `CITED [VERIFY]`; the learner implements a simple ridge-based contextual bandit with exploration bonus on a planted linear generator); **non-stationary** rewards (sliding windows/discounting; drift generators; link `../08-…` Unit 12)); **(3) practical constraints** (delayed and batched feedback; many arms; business constraints (minimum exposure per variant, fairness of exposure: `../09-…` Unit 11); *safety*: never fully starving an arm; bandits for *ranking* and *slates* are harder (named)); **(4) inference after adaptive data collection** (the data are adaptively collected: **sample means can be biased** (exact three-round example: L3: both arm means have expectation `0.4375 < 0.5`); confidence intervals based on i.i.d. theory are invalid; remedies named (`CITED [VERIFY]`): inverse-propensity-weighted/martingale estimators using the logged propensities, adaptive weighting, and anytime-valid confidence sequences (Unit 8)); **(5) off-policy evaluation (OPE) for bandit data** (recalled from 5B-09 Unit 10: IPS, clipped IPS, SNIPS, doubly robust, overlap, effective sample size; **what bandits add**: the *logging policy is the bandit itself*, its propensities are known *if logged* and vary over time (record `π₀(a_t | x_t, history)` at every round); **randomised algorithms (ε-greedy, Thompson sampling) have positive propensities for all arms so OPE is possible; deterministic greedy/UCB logs have propensity 0 for non-chosen arms, which violates positivity** (L3); the **replay** estimator for uniformly random logging (L3); OPE for evaluating a *new* policy before deployment; **policy learning** from logged data by maximising the estimated value with a variance penalty (named: counterfactual risk minimisation `CITED [VERIFY]`)); **(6) when to use which** (A/B tests for estimating the effect of a fixed change with clean inference; bandits when the cost of exposing users to worse arms matters and the goal is performance within the test; **both can run together** (a randomised slice for inference); contextual and personalised allocation when effects are heterogeneous (Unit 6) — **decision-driven, no ranking**); **(7) the feedback loop** (a deployed bandit changes the data it learns from (`../09-…` Unit 11): exploration floors; delayed feedback; non-stationarity; novelty effects; monitoring realised value with a randomised holdout); **(8) simulation as a testing aid, not evidence** (the learner's bandit simulator reproduces regret curves and bias; results are about the planted environments only); **(9) ethics and constraints** (experimenting on users; fairness of exposure and of who bears the cost of exploration; consent and transparency: awareness only); **(10) reporting** (environment, algorithm and parameters, regret with intervals over ≥ 30 seeds, propensity logging, OPE estimates with overlap diagnostics, inference method and its validity, and the decision rationale)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **uniform exploration regret: an A/B test (or any policy that picks arms uniformly) has expected regret `Σ_tΣ_a(1/K)Δ_a = T·Δ̄` with `Δ̄ = (1/K)Σ_aΔ_a`; for arms `(0.5, 0.6)` and `T = 1,000`: `1,000 × 0.05 = 50`** — complete; (b) **ε-greedy with fixed `ε` has linear regret: at every round with probability `ε` the arm is chosen uniformly, contributing expected regret `εΔ̄` per round regardless of how well the arms are known, so `R_T ≥ εΔ̄T`** — complete; (c) **the Hoeffding index: for rewards in `[0, 1]`, `P(μ_a ≥ x̄_{a,n} + ε) ≤ e^{−2nε²}`; with `ε = √(2 ln t/n)` the right side is `e^{−4 ln t} = t^{−4}`: the UCB1 bonus makes the probability that the true mean exceeds the index at time `t` at most `t^{−4}`** — complete (Hoeffding's inequality `CITED`); (d) **Beta–Bernoulli conjugacy: prior `Beta(α, β)`, `s` successes and `f` failures: posterior `∝ θ^{α − 1}(1 − θ)^{β − 1}θ^s(1 − θ)^f = Beta(α + s, β + f)`** — complete; (e) **Thompson sampling selection probability: for two arms with independent posteriors `θ₁ ∼ Beta(2, 1)` (density `2θ`) and `θ₂ ∼ Beta(1, 1)` (uniform), `P(θ₁ > θ₂) = ∫₀¹2θ·θ dθ = 2/3`** — complete (computed); (g) **OPE and positivity for bandit logs: if the logging policy is deterministic given the history (greedy/UCB), then `π₀(a | x) ∈ {0, 1}`; for any target policy placing mass on an arm the logger never chose, positivity fails and the value is not identified (5B-09 Unit 10, L3 (h)); the expected number of usable events is `n/K`** — complete (the matching event is independent of the reward given `(x, a)`); **counterexamples**: **uniform exploration losing `50` successes (computed)**; **ε-greedy with fixed `ε` not converging in regret (seeded: linear regret curve)**; **both arm means biased low after three adaptive rounds (computed `0.4375`)**; **a greedy log with propensity 0 for unchosen arms (seeded; OPE undefined)**; **a Thompson-sampling log whose propensities were not recorded and could not be recovered (design failure)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal ε-greedy, UCB1, Thompson, and LinUCB-style bandits, bandit simulators with regret tracking, replay/IPS/SNIPS/DR estimators, and exact enumeration of tiny adaptive designs vs NumPy (agreement conditions: tie-breaking, prior parameters, propensity definitions)**; adaptive-sampling bias demonstrators; replay and IPS/SNIPS/DR OPE with overlap diagnostics; policy-value scoring against truth; A/B-versus-bandit comparison harness (regret and inference error); spec-literal references;
