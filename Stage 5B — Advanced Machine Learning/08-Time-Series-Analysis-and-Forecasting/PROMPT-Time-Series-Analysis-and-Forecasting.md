## Conventions stated once, used everywhere

- **Claims policy.** Every claim about learning efficacy, hiring, the "best" forecasting method, a window length, a seasonal period, an information-criterion gap, a MASE or coverage cutoff, or what a forecast "shows" is exactly one of: (a) cited research/documentation tagged `[VERIFY]` with limits; (b) practitioner opinion/rule of thumb tagged `[JUDGEMENT]` or `HEURISTIC`; (c) a verifiable statement about mathematics, code, or the tree; or (d) omitted. **No general ranking of forecasting methods; every empirical comparison is stated as evidence about its generator only.** Durations `[ASSUMPTION]`; sizes `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`.
- **Correctness policy.** Every worked example and key is verified **two independent ways**: (1) a hand derivation in the text, and (2) an independent computation — **exact (`Fraction`/`sympy`/enumeration) on the tiny series and on processes with closed-form forecasts wherever possible**, otherwise `numpy`/`scipy`/scikit-learn with a **stated tolerance and reason**, or simulation with stated `T`, seed, replicates, interval. **Every decomposition, baseline, feature builder, forecaster, interval, metric, and backtest in the module is implemented as a short spec-literal reference and cross-checked against pandas/scikit-learn/SciPy (and `statsmodels` only if installed) on tiny and random inputs, including constant series, series shorter than the seasonal period, missing values, ties, and zero values.** A library function is never the sole oracle for a claim about that function.
- **Proof policy.** Where Section 6 says "proof", write it in full: statement, hypotheses, every step justified, closing "where each hypothesis was used". **Heuristics are labelled `HEURISTIC` with their measured behaviour on a stated generator.** Where a result is `CITED [VERIFY]`, say which and give a numerical sanity check.
- **Forecasting-problem convention (stated once; applied everywhere).** **Every forecasting task states: the target series and its unit; the sampling frequency and calendar; the forecast origin `t` and horizon set `h ∈ {1,…,H}`; the information set at the origin (exactly which values and covariates, with their publication lags, are known at `t`; future covariates are used only if they are known in advance, e.g. calendar or planned promotions, and are then labelled "known-future"); the decision the forecast supports (point, interval, or quantile; the cost of over- and under-forecasting); and the cadence at which forecasts are reissued.** Point forecasts are conditional means unless a different functional (median, quantile) is stated; the metric is matched to the functional.
- **Planted-truth convention.** Every decomposition, model, interval, and drift monitor is evaluated on generators for which **the truth is known**: the true components (trend, seasonal, noise); the true ARMA/ETS parameters; the **oracle forecaster that uses the true parameters and its expected h-step loss (for Gaussian linear processes, the h-step error variance `σ²Σψ_j²`)**; the true demand-occurrence process; the true change times; the true hierarchy; so that **forecast error, the gap to the oracle, interval coverage by horizon, parameter-estimation error, and detection delay are measured against truth over ≥ 30 independent generator seeds with intervals**, never inferred from the data the method saw. **The random-walk (and, for seasonal tasks, seasonal-random-walk) null generator is part of every study (the baseline is oracle-optimal there; any method that "beats" it is chance or a leak).**
- **Evaluation-protocol and leak-free convention (inherited from 5A-00 to 5A-13 and the 5B protocol card).** Every learned object — preprocessing and scaling, differencing orders, seasonal periods, features and their windows, hyperparameters, the model, interval calibration, and reconciliation weights — is fitted on rows belonging to its role: **train (fit) / validation (tuning, always later in time than train) / calibration (interval calibration, later than train) / test (sealed; the latest block; touched once)**. **Time order is respected everywhere: no shuffled folds; every rolling or expanding feature at time `t` uses only observations with timestamp ≤ `t − (publication lag)`; no centred windows, backward fills, interpolation that uses future values, or statistics (scalers, seasonal profiles, target encodings) computed over the whole series; hyperparameter tuning is itself rolling-origin (nested in time, 5B-01).** **The unit of independence is stated: forecast origins are serially dependent, series within a panel may be dependent, and effective sample sizes are never the number of origins.** Every example states which items it exercises; **an example that violates one is labelled as a counterexample.**
- **Forecast card (the module's reporting convention; every forecasting result is stated in this form).** **the decision, horizons, and cadence; the series, frequency, calendar, and units; the information set at the origin and the known-future covariates; the baselines run (naive, seasonal naive, drift, mean) and their scores; the backtest design (origins, window type, gap, number of folds, effective sample size); the metrics, their scaling, and the functional they target; the model, its hyperparameters, and how they were selected (nested in time); the interval method and its measured coverage by horizon; the oracle gap on planted generators; the leak checks; the drift monitors and retraining policy; the seeds, budget, and number of looks.** Reporting a forecast score without a baseline, a horizon, and the number of origins is a defect; every worked example ends with the filled card.

## Unit 1 — Trend, seasonality, and residuals

- **L1:** **a time series is a sequence of values ordered in time, and *order matters*: today's value is related to yesterday's. Three ideas describe most business series: *trend* (slow long-run movement), *seasonality* (a pattern that repeats with a fixed period `m`: 7 for daily data with a weekly cycle, 12 for monthly data with a yearly cycle), and *residual* (what is left)**; **additive model `y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t` (seasonal swings of constant size) versus multiplicative `y_t = T_t × S_t × R_t` (swings proportional to the level; taking logarithms turns it into an additive model)**; **a first worked example by hand: a twelve-point table with a linear trend and a period-4 seasonal pattern: estimate the trend by a centred moving average, subtract it, average the detrended values by position in the cycle to get the seasonal indices (which sum to zero), and read the residuals (computed exactly)**; **what "stationary" means in plain words (the series' average level and variability do not drift, and the relation between values depends only on how far apart they are), why most forecasting models want it, and how *differencing* (`y_t − y_{t−1}`) can remove a trend**; **common beginner mistakes: reading a trend into a random walk; fitting a seasonal pattern with the wrong period; decomposing on the whole series and then forecasting as if the future were known**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **decomposition and diagnostics in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the components and their definitions** (level, trend, one or two seasonal periods, calendar/holiday effects, residual; additive versus multiplicative and when a log transform makes the seasonal amplitude constant (measured: the coefficient of variation of the seasonal swing by level); the Box–Cox family named); **(2) classical decomposition** (the centred moving average of order `m` (for even `m`, a `2 × m` average with half weights at the ends); seasonal indices by period position, normalised to sum to zero (additive) or to `m` (multiplicative); the from-scratch implementation versus `statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.seasonal_decompose` only if installed; end-effects: the trend estimate is undefined for the first and last `⌊m/2⌋` points (a *forecasting* limitation: the decomposition cannot be used at the origin without a model for the trend)); **(3) STL-style decomposition** (named; the idea: iterated loess smoothing of the seasonal subseries and the trend; robust weights; the learner implements a simplified fixed-seasonality version and states what it omits; **no claim of equality with a library**); **(4) differencing and transformation** (first difference removes a linear trend; seasonal difference `∇_m` removes an exactly periodic component; order of differencing; **overdifferencing** introduces a unit-root MA component (L3) and inflates variance: measured on a white-noise series); **(5) stationarity and unit roots** (weak stationarity; random walk versus trend-stationary; **spurious regression: two independent random walks regressed on each other show a high `R²` and "significant" slope far more often than the nominal level (measured by simulation; `CITED [VERIFY]` Granger–Newbold)**; unit-root tests (ADF, KPSS) named with their null hypotheses and low power against near-unit-root alternatives (measured on the generators); the practical rule: stationarity is a modelling assumption to test by forecasting performance, not a switch)); **(6) autocorrelation and residual diagnostics** (sample ACF `r_k`; for white noise `r_k ≈ N(0, 1/T)` so the ±1.96/√T band covers each lag with probability ≈ 0.95 but across `h` lags the chance of at least one exceedance is `1 − 0.95^h` (`h = 20`: `0.642`); the Ljung–Box statistic `Q = T(T + 2)Σ_{k=1}^{h} r_k²/(T − k)` ≈ `χ²_{h − (p + q)}` under the null (`CITED [VERIFY]`; a seeded size check); a residual-diagnostics checklist: no pattern in the residual plot, ACF inside the bands overall, constant variance, approximate normality for interval validity, no relation to covariates); **(7) multiple and changing seasonality** (daily + weekly + yearly; Fourier terms preview (Unit 3); changing seasonal amplitude; calendar effects and moving holidays); **(8) outliers, level shifts, and structural breaks** (additive outliers versus level shifts versus slope changes: how each looks in the differenced series; robust decomposition; the link to drift (Unit 12)); **(9) decomposition for forecasting versus for description** (a decomposition fitted on the whole series is *descriptive* and uses the future; a forecast needs the components estimated at the origin only: the leak-free rule); **(10) reporting** (frequency and `m`, transformation, decomposition method, what was estimated on which rows, residual diagnostics with the multiplicity caveat, and what the decomposition cannot say)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **the `m`-point centred moving average annihilates a seasonal component and preserves a linear trend: let `s_t` have period `m` with `Σ_{j=1}^{m} s_j = 0`; for odd `m = 2k + 1`, `Σ_{i=−k}^{k} s_{t+i}` is a sum over one full period (any `m` consecutive values), hence `0`; for even `m` the `2 × m` average with weights `(½, 1, …, 1, ½)/m` over `m + 1` terms equals the average of two consecutive `m`-term period sums, hence `0`; for a linear trend `a + bt` the symmetric weights give `a + bt` exactly (the weighted mean of `t + i` over symmetric weights is `t`)** — complete; (b) **differencing: if `y_t` is a polynomial of degree `d` in `t`, then `∇^d y_t` is constant and `∇^{d+1} y_t = 0` (induction: `∇` lowers the degree by one); `∇_m s_t = 0` for any period-`m` sequence** — complete; (c) **overdifferencing: if `y_t = ε_t` is white noise (variance `σ²`), then `x_t = ∇y_t = ε_t − ε_{t−1}` has `Var(x_t) = 2σ²`, `Cov(x_t, x_{t−1}) = −σ²`, `ρ_1 = −½`, and is an MA(1) with `θ = −1` (non-invertible); the variance doubles and the series acquires a spurious negative autocorrelation** — complete; (d) **the chance of a spurious ACF exceedance: for `h` independent lag tests each with exceedance probability `0.05`, `P(at least one) = 1 − 0.95^h`; `h = 20` gives `0.642` (computed); the lag-`k` sample autocorrelations of white noise are uncorrelated at leading order (`CITED [VERIFY]`), so independence is an approximation checked by simulation** — complete for the arithmetic, `CITED` for the approximation; (e) **log transformation: if `y_t = T_tS_tR_t` with positive factors then `ln y_t = ln T_t + ln S_t + ln R_t`; if the seasonal swing is a fixed percentage `p` of the level then the additive swing in levels equals `p·T_t` (grows with the level) while in logs it is the constant `ln(1 + p)`-type amplitude (compute for `p = 10%`: `0.0953`)** — complete; (f) **end-effects: the centred moving average of order `m` is defined only for `t ∈ [⌊m/2⌋ + 1, T − ⌊m/2⌋]`; a decomposition therefore has no trend estimate at the origin, and a trend estimated with a one-sided window lags a linear trend by `(m − 1)/2` steps (compute: a trailing mean of a linear trend equals the trend at `t − (m − 1)/2`)** — complete; **counterexamples**: **two independent random walks with spurious `R² > 0.5` in a large share of seeded pairs (measured with interval)**; **a random-walk series "decomposed" into a trend and a seasonal pattern that does not exist (seeded)**; **a wrong period `m` producing a seasonal component that explains nothing (computed)**; **overdifferenced white noise with `ρ₁ = −0.5` (computed)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal classical decomposition, differencing, ACF/PACF and Ljung–Box vs pandas, SciPy, and `statsmodels` (only if installed) (agreement conditions: centring, normalisation, `ddof`, `nlags`)**; **tool card** for `forecastlab.decomp` (planted trend+seasonal+noise generators with true components; centred and trailing moving averages; classical and simplified STL-style decomposition; differencing and seasonal differencing; ACF/PACF/Ljung–Box with multiplicity notes; spurious-regression demonstrators; leak-free (origin-only) decomposition mode; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).
- **L4:** **decomposition in production (an MLOps lens)**: **calendars and frequencies as data-contract fields (a series declares its frequency, timezone, and calendar; unsorted or duplicated timestamps are rejected); seasonal-period detection as a monitored assumption (periodogram or ACF peak, with a stability check across windows); recomputing components on schedule with the origin-only rule; residual diagnostics as a post-deployment health check (link 5D `12`); documenting transformations (log/Box–Cox) and their back-transformation bias (the mean of exp(forecast) is not the forecast of the mean: stated)**; hostile inputs (series shorter than `2m`; constant series; zeros and negatives under a log transform; `NaN` runs; duplicate timestamps; daylight-saving shifts; leap days; mixed frequencies); **exhaustive verification** of the moving-average annihilation and trend preservation **over all small seasonal patterns and periods (exact `Fraction`)**, of the differencing claims **over all small polynomial coefficients**, and of the overdifferencing moments **by exact enumeration of small noise sequences** (domains named and sized; `EVIDENCE ONLY`); mutation lab (decomposition fitted on the whole series before forecasting; seasonal indices not normalised; period off by one; a trailing mean treated as centred; log back-transformation ignored; ACF bands read without multiplicity; differencing applied twice silently) with no surviving mutants; case study: a seasonality "discovered" with the wrong period in a random-walk series, and a decomposition fitted on the full history that made a backtest look excellent (original synthetic reproductions with origin-only fits and a baseline as fixes).

## Unit 2 — Naive and seasonal-naive baselines

- **L1:** **a *baseline* is a simple forecast you must beat to justify anything more complex: *naive* (tomorrow = today), *seasonal naive* (this Monday = last Monday), *mean* (always the historical average), and *drift* (extend the line from the first to the last point)**; **the rule: run the baselines first, on the same backtest, with the same metrics; a model that does not beat seasonal naive on a seasonal series is not ready**; **a first worked example by hand: a twelve-point table: the four baseline forecasts for the next 4 points, their absolute errors against the four held-out points, the mean absolute error of each, and the in-sample naive MAE used to scale them (the MASE; computed exactly)**; **which baseline is "right" depends on the series: for pure noise the mean is best, for a random walk naive is best, for a strongly seasonal series seasonal naive is best**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **baselines and scaled metrics in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the four baselines** (naive `ŷ_{T+h} = y_T`; seasonal naive `ŷ_{T+h} = y_{T+h−m⌈h/m⌉}`; mean; drift `y_T + h(y_T − y_1)/(T − 1)`; the *seasonal drift* variant; moving-average and median baselines named); **(2) when each is optimal** (naive for a random walk; seasonal naive for a seasonal random walk; mean for iid noise; drift for a random walk with drift: each proved in L3; **the oracle-optimality is why the random-walk null generator belongs in every study**); **(3) error measures** (ME/bias, MAE, MSE/RMSE, MAPE, sMAPE: definitions and failure cases (zeros, asymmetry); **scaled errors**: `MASE = mean|e_t| / (in-sample mean |y_t − y_{t−1}|)` (or the seasonal-naive scale for seasonal data), `RMSSE`; interpretation: `MASE < 1` means better than the in-sample one-step naive; **the scale is computed on the training window only (leak-free)**); **(4) comparing to baselines honestly** (the baseline's error at each horizon; relative errors and skill scores `1 − MSE_model/MSE_baseline`; the benchmark must use the same origins, horizons, and information set; a model that wins at `h = 1` and loses at `h = 12` is a different model for a different decision); **(5) the horizon structure of baseline error** (naive error variance grows linearly with `h` for a random walk; seasonal naive error does not grow within the same seasonal position; a measured table by horizon on generators); **(6) choosing a baseline for a seasonal series** (period selection; multiple seasonality; holiday effects: seasonal naive with calendar alignment (e.g., same weekday last year) as a named alternative); **(7) baselines for panels** (per-series baselines; pooled "last value × seasonal profile"; the cost of the baseline sweep in counts); **(8) baselines as monitors** (a production forecaster is monitored against its baseline: relative error tracked with intervals; link `../06-…`); **(9) when a baseline is not enough** (long horizons with trend changes; covariate-driven series; interventions: named); **(10) reporting** (the baselines, scales, horizons, origins, and intervals; always the baseline row first)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **naive is optimal for a random walk: for `y_t = y_{t−1} + ε_t` with white-noise `ε`, the minimum-MSE `h`-step forecast given `y_1,…,y_T` is `E[y_{T+h} | y_{1:T}] = y_T`, with error variance `hσ²`** — complete (the conditional mean of a sum of independent zero-mean increments); (b) **the mean forecast for iid noise: for `y_t = μ + ε_t`, the forecast `ȳ_T` has error `ε_{T+h} − (ȳ_T − μ)` with variance `σ²(1 + 1/T)`, while the naive forecast has error variance `2σ²`: naive is worse by a factor `2/(1 + 1/T)` (`T = 20`: `1.905`)** — complete; (c) **drift: `ŷ_{T+h} = y_T + h·(y_T − y_1)/(T − 1)` for a random walk with drift has error `Σ_{j=1}^{h}ε_{T+j} − h·Σ_{t=2}^{T}ε_t/(T − 1)`, so `Var = hσ²(1 + h/(T − 1))` (`h = 4`, `T = 25`: `4.667σ²` vs naive `4σ²` when the true drift is zero); the extra variance is the price of estimating the drift** — complete; (d) **seasonal naive is optimal for a seasonal random walk `y_t = y_{t−m} + ε_t`: error variance `⌈h/m⌉σ²`** — complete; (e) **MASE scale and interpretation: for a random walk with `Var(ε) = σ²` the expected in-sample one-step naive absolute error is `σ·√(2/π)` (Gaussian); an out-of-sample one-step naive forecast therefore has `MASE ≈ 1` in expectation (hypotheses stated); a `h`-step naive forecast has `MASE ≈ √h`** — complete (the mean absolute value of `N(0, hσ²)`); (f) **MAPE and the low-biased optimum: for `y ∈ {50, 150}` with equal probability, the MAPE-minimising constant forecast over `f ∈ [50, 150]` has derivative `½/50 − ½/150 > 0`, so the minimiser is `f = 50` while the MSE-minimiser is `100`; the MAPE-optimal forecast is biased low** — complete (computed); **counterexamples**: **naive beating a fitted AR model on a random-walk generator in most seeds (seeded; share with interval)**; **mean forecast beating naive on iid noise (computed factor 1.905 at `T = 20`)**; **a MAPE leaderboard that ranks the biased-low forecast first (computed)**; **a MASE scale computed on the whole series, leaking the test window (seeded)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal baselines and scaled metrics vs pandas/scikit-learn (agreement conditions: seasonal alignment, zero handling, scale window)**; **tool card** for `forecastlab.baselines` (naive, seasonal naive, mean, drift, seasonal drift; scaled and unscaled metrics with training-window scales; skill scores with intervals; horizon-profile tables; random-walk and seasonal-random-walk null generators with oracle errors; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).

## Unit 3 — Lag features and rolling statistics

- **L1:** **to use a regression model on a time series, turn the series into a table: each row is a time point, the target is the value at that time, and the features are *lags* (values 1, 2, 7 steps earlier), *rolling statistics* (the average or standard deviation of the last 7 values), and *calendar features* (day of week, month, holiday flag)**; **the golden rule: every feature for a row at time `t` may use only information available *before* the forecast was made — a rolling mean that includes today's value, or a centred window that peeks at tomorrow, leaks the answer**; **a first worked example by hand: a twelve-point series: the lag-1, lag-2, and lag-4 columns (the first rows are missing: rows lost), a trailing 3-point rolling mean that excludes the current value (`shift(1)` first), and the same mean computed centred (which contains `y_{t+1}`) — the leaky version's correlation with the target compared with the honest one (computed exactly)**; **why shuffled cross-validation is wrong for these tables: rows are not independent, and neighbours leak into each other**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 4 — Forecast horizons: recursive versus direct

- **L1:** **a forecast is made at an *origin* for a *horizon*: "the demand 1, 2, …, 14 days from now". There are two basic ways to forecast several steps ahead with a regression model: *recursive* (train one model for the next step; to go further, feed its own forecast back in as if it were an observation) and *direct* (train a separate model for each horizon, each predicting `h` steps ahead from information at the origin)**; **a first worked example by hand: an AR(1) series with `φ = 0.8` and last value 10: the recursive forecasts `8, 6.4, 5.12, 4.096` (each `0.8 ×` the previous) and the direct forecasts using the horizon-specific coefficients `0.8, 0.64, 0.512, 0.4096` — identical when the model is right (computed exactly) — then a case where they differ because the one-step model is misspecified**; **trade-offs in plain words: recursive uses data efficiently but errors compound; direct avoids feeding forecasts back but needs a model per horizon and ignores the link between horizons**; **forecast error grows with the horizon — report it by horizon**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **multi-step strategies in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the strategies** (**recursive/iterated**; **direct** (one model per horizon `h`, each with target `y_{t+h}` and features at `t`); **MIMO** (one model with a vector output); **DirRec** (direct with previous horizons' forecasts as features: named); a **hybrid** by horizon band; **multi-horizon neural approaches are 5C: named**); **(2) the recursive strategy's compounding** (the h-step error variance of an AR(1) forecast `σ²(1 − φ^{2h})/(1 − φ²)` (derived in L3) rises from `σ²` toward the process variance; with an *estimated and misspecified* one-step model, bias also compounds: a measured study on a long-memory-style generator; the recursive strategy needs forecasts of *all* features it feeds back (exogenous covariates must be known-future or separately forecast)); **(3) the direct strategy** (the training table for horizon `h` has `T − L − h + 1` rows (Unit 3); **fit count `H` models per refit** (counted before running); no error compounding, but no consistency across horizons (forecasts can be non-smooth in `h`: a measured example); smoothing across horizons as a regulariser: named); **(4) bias–variance trade-off between strategies** (recursive: low variance, bias if the one-step model is misspecified; direct: higher variance (a different noisy fit per horizon), robust to misspecification: derived in L3 on AR(1) versus MA(1); the *measured* comparison on the planted generators as evidence about those generators only — **no ranking**); **(5) choosing the horizon set and what to report** (the decision's horizons; cumulative versus point-in-time targets (total demand over a lead time versus demand on day `h`); errors reported by `h` with intervals, never pooled across horizons without weights); **(6) lead time and the decision** (inventory: the demand over the lead time `h` is the sum of `h` daily demands: its forecast variance is not `h` times the one-day variance under autocorrelation (derived for AR(1)); a direct model for the cumulative target is natural); **(7) exogenous inputs** (known-future versus unknown covariates; scenario inputs; the cost of forecast errors in covariates); **(8) refit and reissue cadence** (rolling forecasts reissued daily; each issue's `H` horizons; the *forecast revision* series as a monitor); **(9) evaluating multi-step forecasts** (per-horizon backtests (Unit 11); the origin set shared across strategies; paired comparison by origin with the Diebold–Mariano test and the autocorrelation caveat (Unit 11)); **(10) reporting** (strategy, horizons, models per refit, fit counts, errors by horizon with intervals, baseline by horizon)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **AR(1) forecast: for `y_t = φy_{t−1} + ε_t`, `|φ| < 1`, the optimal `h`-step forecast is `φ^hy_T` and the error is `Σ_{j=0}^{h−1}φ^jε_{T+h−j}` with variance `σ²(1 − φ^{2h})/(1 − φ²)`; the direct regression of `y_{t+h}` on `y_t` has population slope `Cov(y_{t+h}, y_t)/Var(y_t) = ρ_h = φ^h`** — complete; (c) **misspecification favours direct: let `y_t = ε_t + θε_{t−1}` (MA(1), `ρ_1 = θ/(1 + θ²)`, `ρ_h = 0` for `h ≥ 2`); the best-linear one-lag model has slope `ρ_1`; the recursive two-step forecast uses slope `ρ_1²`, the direct two-step slope is `ρ_2 = 0`; the excess two-step MSE of the recursive forecast is `ρ_1⁴γ_0`; ## Unit 5 — Autoregression and moving-average intuition

- **L1:** **an *autoregressive* model AR(p) says today's value is a weighted sum of the last `p` values plus fresh noise: `y_t = c + φ₁y_{t−1} + … + φ_py_{t−p} + ε_t`; a *moving-average* model MA(q) says today's value is a weighted sum of the last `q` *noise shocks*: `y_t = μ + ε_t + θ₁ε_{t−1} + … + θ_qε_{t−q}`**; **intuition: AR models have a memory that fades gradually (a shock echoes forever, shrinking each step); MA models have a memory that cuts off sharply (a shock affects only the next `q` values)**; **a first worked example by hand: AR(1) with `φ = 0.8`: the autocorrelations `0.8, 0.64, 0.512, …`; MA(1) with `θ = 0.5`: `ρ₁ = 0.5/1.25 = 0.4` and `ρ_k = 0` for `k ≥ 2` (computed exactly); then reading which of the two a given ACF table looks like**; **stationarity in plain words for AR(1): the model is stable only if `|φ| < 1`; at `φ = 1` it is a random walk (Unit 2)**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **AR and MA models in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) the models and the lag operator** (`φ(B)y_t = c + θ(B)ε_t`; white noise; the role of the mean/intercept; AR(1) as a weighted sum of past shocks (MA(∞)); MA(1) as an AR(∞) when invertible); **(2) stationarity and invertibility** (AR(p) is stationary iff all roots of `φ(z) = 1 − φ₁z − … − φ_pz^p` lie outside the unit circle; for AR(2) the triangle `φ₂ + φ₁ < 1`, `φ₂ − φ₁ < 1`, `|φ₂| < 1` (L3); MA(q) is always stationary and is *invertible* iff the roots of `θ(z)` are outside the unit circle; **why invertibility is imposed: two different MA(1) parameters (`θ` and `1/θ`) give the same autocorrelations (L3), so the model is unidentified without it**); **(3) ACF and PACF signatures** (AR(p): ACF decays, PACF cuts off after lag `p`; MA(q): ACF cuts off after lag `q`, PACF decays; mixed ARMA: both decay; the *partial* autocorrelation as the last coefficient of the best AR fit of each order; sampling variability: ±1.96/√T bands and the multiplicity caveat of Unit 1; **identification by eye is a heuristic with measured accuracy on generators, not a procedure**); **(4) estimation** (**Yule–Walker** from sample autocovariances (method of moments; a Toeplitz system: solved by Levinson–Durbin, `O(p²)`: the learner implements it); **least squares on lagged regressors** (conditional least squares; the same model as the Unit 3 lag-feature regression); **maximum likelihood** for Gaussian AR(p) (exact versus conditional likelihood; Unit 6); MA estimation is non-linear (innovations algorithm / numerical likelihood: Unit 6); bias of AR coefficient estimates in small samples (`E[φ̂] ≈ φ − (1 + 3φ)/T` for AR(1) with a mean estimated, `CITED [VERIFY]`; measured by simulation: the downward bias and its effect on multi-step forecasts)); **(5) forecasting with AR and MA** (AR: recursive substitution (Unit 4); MA(q): forecasts beyond `q` steps equal the mean; **MA forecasts need estimated past shocks** (innovations), which are residuals of the fitted model; error variances from ψ-weights (Unit 6)); **(6) model order selection** (information criteria AIC/AICc/BIC (definitions; AIC targets prediction, BIC consistency: `CITED [VERIFY]`); order selection by rolling-origin validation instead; measured over-/under-fitting rates on the planted generators — evidence only); **(7) diagnosing a fitted AR/MA** (residual ACF, Ljung–Box with degrees-of-freedom adjustment, plots as text); **(8) ARMA as a parsimonious compromise** (an ARMA(1,1) can mimic a long AR; identification problems with common factors (cancelling roots): a computed example); **(9) AR models as lag-feature regressions and what they add** (the coefficients have a stationarity structure; extrapolation is stable; the regression view lets you add covariates (ARX) and regularise); **(10) reporting** (order, estimation method, stationarity/invertibility check, residual diagnostics, forecast and error by horizon, rolling-origin score versus baseline)**.
- **L3:** **proofs, written in full:** (a) **AR(1): for `|φ| < 1`, `y_t = Σ_{j≥0}φ^jε_{t−j}` (mean-square convergent), `Var(y_t) = σ²/(1 − φ²)`, `γ_k = σ²φ^k/(1 − φ²)`, `ρ_k = φ^k`; for `|φ| ≥ 1` no stationary solution with finite variance exists (the variance recursion `γ₀ = φ²γ₀ + σ²` has no positive solution)** — complete; (b) **MA(1): `γ₀ = σ²(1 + θ²)`, `γ₁ = σ²θ`, `γ_k = 0` for `k ≥ 2`; `ρ₁ = θ/(1 + θ²)` satisfies `|ρ₁| ≤ ½` with equality iff `|θ| = 1` (AM–GM: `1 + θ² ≥ 2|θ|`)** — complete; (c) **non-identifiability: `θ` and `1/θ` give the same `ρ₁` (`(1/θ)/(1 + 1/θ²) = θ/(θ² + 1)`); for `θ = 0.5` and `θ = 2`, `ρ₁ = 0.4` for both; the invertible representative (`|θ| < 1`) is chosen** — complete (computed); (d) **AR(2) stationarity region: the roots of `1 − φ₁z − φ₂z²` lie outside the unit circle iff `φ₁ + φ₂ < 1`, `φ₂ − φ₁ < 1`, `|φ₂| < 1` (Schur–Cohn/Jury conditions for a quadratic; derive by requiring `|z₁z₂| = 1/|φ₂| > 1` and the sign conditions of the polynomial at `z = ±1`)** — complete; (e) **Yule–Walker: multiplying the AR(p) equation by `y_{t−k}` and taking expectations gives `γ_k = Σ_jφ_jγ_{k−j}` for `k ≥ 1`; for AR(2): `ρ₁ = φ₁/(1 − φ₂)`, `ρ₂ = φ₁ρ₁ + φ₂`; inverse: `φ₁ = ρ₁(1 − ρ₂)/(1 − ρ₁²)`, `φ₂ = (ρ₂ − ρ₁²)/(1 − ρ₁²)` (compute for `φ = (0.5, 0.3)`: `ρ₁ = 0.714`, `ρ₂ = 0.657`)** — complete; (f) **PACF cut-off: for AR(p) the lag-`k` partial autocorrelation (the last coefficient of the order-`k` Yule–Walker solution) is zero for `k > p` because the order-`p` coefficients already satisfy the order-`k` equations with zeros appended (uniqueness of the Toeplitz solution)** — complete; (g) **ψ-weights and forecast variance: writing `y_t = Σ_jψ_jε_{t−j}` (`ψ₀ = 1`), the optimal `h`-step forecast error is `Σ_{j=0}^{h−1}ψ_jε_{T+h−j}` with variance `σ²Σ_{j<h}ψ_j²`; for AR(1) `ψ_j = φ^j`; for MA(1) `ψ₁ = θ`, `ψ_j = 0` after, so the error variance stops growing at `h = 2`: `σ²(1 + θ²)`** — complete; **counterexamples**: **two MA(1) models with identical ACF (computed `θ = 0.5` versus `2`)**; **an AR(2) outside the triangle producing an explosive path (seeded)**; **an ACF read as MA(2) that is actually AR(1) at small `T` (seeded; identification error rate)**; **the downward small-sample AR bias shortening multi-step forecasts (seeded)**; **numerical analysis**: **spec-literal ACF/PACF, Yule–Walker with Levinson–Durbin, conditional least squares, and ψ-weights vs SciPy/NumPy and `statsmodels` (only if installed) (agreement conditions: mean removal, `ddof`, biased versus unbiased autocovariance)**; **tool card** for `forecastlab.arma` (AR/MA/ARMA simulators with exact autocovariances and ψ-weights; ACF/PACF; Yule–Walker and Levinson–Durbin; conditional least squares; stationarity and invertibility checkers; order-selection studies with rolling-origin scoring; oracle forecasters and error variances; spec-literal references; stable errors; no input mutation).

## Unit 6 — ARIMA and SARIMA concepts

## Unit 7 — Exponential smoothing and Prophet-style models

- **L1:** **exponential smoothing forecasts the next value as a weighted average of past values, with weights that decay geometrically — recent values count more: `level_t = α·y_t + (1 − α)·level_{t−1}`; the forecast is the last level. *Holt's* method adds a trend component, *Holt–Winters* adds seasonality; *damped* trends flatten out over long horizons**; **a first worked example by hand: simple exponential smoothing on a six-point series with `α = 0.5`: every level update, the one-step forecasts, the weights `0.5, 0.25, 0.125, …`, and the effect of `α = 0.1` versus `0.9` (computed exactly); then Holt's method on a trending series**; **a *Prophet-style* model is an additive regression: a piecewise-linear (or logistic) trend with changepoints + Fourier seasonal terms + holiday effects, fitted by regularised regression — flexible and easy to use, but it is *a regression on time*, not a model of short-term dependence**; ≥ 3 worked examples.

## Unit 9 — Global models and hierarchical forecasting

## Unit 10 — Probabilistic forecasting and forecast intervals

- **L1:** **a point forecast says "120 units"; a *probabilistic* forecast says "80% chance the demand is between 95 and 148" — which is what a stocking or capacity decision needs. A *prediction interval* is a range intended to contain the future value with a stated probability; *quantile forecasts* give the value below which a stated share of outcomes falls (the 10th, 50th, 90th percentiles)**; **intervals get *wider* as the horizon grows, because uncertainty accumulates; an interval whose width does not grow with the horizon is probably wrong**; **a first worked example by hand: a random-walk series with a residual standard deviation of 2: the 80% interval half-widths at horizons 1 to 4 (`1.2816 × 2 × √h`: `2.56, 3.63, 4.44, 5.13`) and what happens if the one-step width is reused at horizon 4 (the true coverage is only 47.8%, computed)**; **how to check an interval: over many past forecasts, count how often the actual value fell inside; an "80%" interval that covers 60% is overconfident**; ≥ 3 worked examples.
- **L2:** **forecast intervals and probabilistic forecasting in depth (derived/measured)**: **(1) sources of forecast uncertainty** (future innovations; parameter estimation; model selection and misspecification; ## Unit 11 — Rolling-origin backtesting and forecast metrics

## Unit 12 — Missing timestamps, intermittent demand, and drift

- **L1:** **real series have holes and irregularities: missing timestamps (a day with no record), duplicated timestamps, irregular spacing, daylight-saving shifts.
